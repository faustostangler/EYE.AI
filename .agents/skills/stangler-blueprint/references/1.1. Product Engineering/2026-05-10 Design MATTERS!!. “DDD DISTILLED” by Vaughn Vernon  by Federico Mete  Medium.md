DDD is a set of tools that assist you in designing and implementing software.When you doubt that design matters, remember Steve Job’s insights: “Most people make the mistake of thinking design is what it looks like […] It’s not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works ”.
Without a proper design, developers may end up adding business logic in user interface components or persistence operations in the middle of business logic, building strongly coupled components. In addition, as they probably collaborate poorly with the business, they don’t give proper emphasis to naming objects and operations, according to the business purpose they fill. They also may be spending lot of time generalizing solutions rather than addressing actual concrete business needs. Decisions like these happens all the time and make systems very, VERY, difficult to maintain. Still, many software development teams don’t give design  even   a passing thought, probably due to the pressure to deliver, without realizing that the cost to the business is really high.
A bounded context and an ubiquitous language
DDD is primarily about modeling a solution in an explicitly bounded context, that supports integration with other bounded contexts**.** Within this boundary, each component of the model has a specific meaning, and does specific things. This model uses an ubiquitous language, a language that is developed and spoken by the team members, and any of them understands what is meant with precision and constraints. As you cross a boundary, the official language changes and it’s possible that people from other teams would have a different meaning for the same terminology.
Ideally, there should be one team  assigned to work on one bounded context, with a separate ubiquitous language, a separate source code and a separate database schema.
Context should hold closely all concepts  that are core to the strategic initiative and push out all others, to avoid the design of monolithic applications. What is core? We should start with domain experts’ mental model, as the will naturally be more focused on business concerns. On the other hand, developers that are focused on software development must resist the urge to be so technically centered and have to dig into  the business model with domain experts (reject any tendency to allow documents to rule over conversation!!). Business’s departments or work group divisions can provide a good indication of where model boundaries should exist. You can determine that this kind of segregation is needed when you consider that each business function likely has different definitions for the same term.
The core domain  may be very high on your organization’s list of projects, and demarcates where it must excel; it’s where the organization needs to invest most liberally in software. When model grows, as new concepts adhere to the ubiquitous language, some modeling concepts should be removed and composed into their own respective bounded contexts, each adhering to its own ubiquitous language.
Domain and subdomains models
Domain  can be expressed as a set of concrete scenarios about what your model is supposed to do. Be careful about the time spent in your domain-modeling efforts when it comes to keeping documents, as in the end, the code is the model and the model is the code. A refined scenario looks like this: “The product owner commits a backlog item to a sprint. The backlog item may be committed only if it is already scheduled for release, and if a quorum of team members have approved commitment. If it is already committed to a different sprint, it must be uncommitted first. When the commitment completes, notify the sprint from which it was uncommitted and the sprint to which it is now committed”. After defining the scenarios, you should put them to work, creating artifacts that can be used to validate your domain model against the team’s specifications. This technique, called specification by example or BDD (Behavior-Driven Development), consist in using a tool to execute the specifications, authoring with its given/when/then approach. Another way is to use a unit testing framework to accomplish much the same thing, where you create acceptance tests (not unit tests) that validate your domain model. Most domain experts should be able to follow this code with some help from the developer.
In addition to the domain model, that should be free of technology, bounded context  may be composed of other layers, as:
Input adapters, such as user interface controllers, REST endpoints, and message listeners.
Application services, that orchestrate use cases and manage transactions.
Output adapters, such as persistence management and message senders.
A subdomain is a sub-part of your overall business domain and can be used to logically break up your whole business domain; it is a clear area of expertise, that will have one or more domain experts. There are different types of subdomains:
Core domain
Supporting subdomain: it’s a custom development, as an off-the-shelf solution doesn’t exist. You may want to consider outsourcing this kind of bounded context.
Generic Subdomain: it may be available for purchase off the shelf.
Even most optimal modeling composition implies one subdomain per bounded context and one bounded context per subdomain, there may be multiple subdomains in one bounded context. When multiple tangled models  don’t have explicit boundaries you could end up with a “ big ball of mud ”, a monolith, where multiple teams work on. It’s often the result of unbridled effort made by a team of software developers who don’t listen to the business experts. These models, that should have been separately designed and implemented, were jumbled together into one very   complex and intertwined mess.
Integrating with others
Core Domain or other subdomains would have to integrate with other bounded contexts. That integration is known in DDD as context mapping. This includes inter-team dynamic between the two bounded contexts and translation between the two ubiquitous languages, or in some other way adapting. There are several kinds of context mappings, both team and technical:
Team integrations
Partnership: This relationship exists between two teams to align both with a dependent set of goals. They will meet frequently, and probably succeed or fail together, and should work together only as long as it provides an advantage. They may have to use continuous integration to keep their integrations in harmony.
Shared Kernel: The teams share a small but common model which they must agree on. They must have open communication, and constant agreement. It’s difficult to maintain.
Upstream-Downstream:
Customer-Supplier: The supplier (upstream) must provide what the customer (downstream) needs, but he ends up determining what really and when.
Conformist: The upstream team has no motivation to support the specific needs of the downstream team. A team will often become a conformist when integrating with a very large and complex model that is well established.
Technical integrations
Anticorruption Layer: The most defensive context mapping relationship, where the downstream team creates a translation layer between its model and the model that is upstream to it. This keep downstream completely isolated foreign concepts. The cost could be too high in various ways for some cases. Try to create an Anticorruption Layer against each legacy system
Open Host Service: It is a reversal of the anticorruption layer pattern**.** This protocol is “open” so that all who need to integrate with your bounded context can use it. The API is convenient for the consumers, well documented, and easy to consume.
To integrate with a given bounded context It could be RPC via SOAP or RESTful interfaces, or it could be a messaging interface using queues or publish-subscribe (database or file system integration should be avoided):
RPC: It implies strong coupling between a client bounded context and the bounded context providing the service. It can lack robustness.
RESTful HTTP: A service bounded context that sports a REST interface should provide an open host service and a published language. Resources should be designed synthetically to follow client-driven use cases and must have the shape and composition of what they need, not what the actual domain model looks like.
Messaging: It is accomplished by a client bounded context subscribing to the domain events published by your own or another bounded context. It is one of the most robust forms of integration. The messaging mechanism should support at-least-once delivery to ensure that all messages will be received eventually. In that case, the messaging mechanism will periodically redeliver a given message and the receiver should be designed to deal correctly with this situation, ignoring the repeated message, or safely re-applying the operation (idempotent receiver). Using enriched events allows for greater autonomy of dependent consumers, but if it is difficult to predict every piece of data that all consumers will ever need, designing a rich query model may be the choice you need. Sometimes circumstances will call for a balanced blend of both approaches.
Aggregates: Grouping entities
They are the concepts that live inside a bounded context. An aggregate is compose of entities (individual unique things) and value objects (don’t have an unique identity and are often used to describe an entity). One entity is called the aggregate root  and owns all the other elements clustered inside it; the name of the root entity is the aggregate ’s conceptual name. An aggregate forms a transactional consistency boundary, where all composed parts must be consistent, according to business rules. Some rules of thumb:
Protect business invariants inside aggregate boundaries. The business should ultimately determine aggregate compositions based on what must be consistent when a transaction is committed.
Design small aggregates. The memory footprint and transactional scope of each aggregate should be relatively small. If your aggregate is trying to do too many things, it is not following SRP, and this will likely be telling in its size.
Reference other aggregates by identity only. This helps keep aggregates small and prevents reaching out to modify multiple Aggregates in the same transaction. Implies lower memory requirements and quicker loading.
Update other aggregates using eventual consistency
Avoid the anemic domain model. That’s when all of your aggregates have only public accessors (getters and setters) but no real business behavior (When using functional programming, the rules change considerably.
because functional programming promotes the separation of data and behavior; data is designed as immutable and behavior is implemented as pure functions)
If any other aggregates you have defined must be updated in reaction  to changes made to an aggregate A1, make a list of each; then, ask the domain experts how much time may elapse until each of the reaction-based updates may take place; it could (a) immediately, or (b) within N seconds/minutes/hours/days. For each of the immediate, consider composing those two entities within the same aggregate boundary. However, it is very unlikely that the business really needs immediate consistency in every case, and it’s ok with eventual consistency.
To implement technically a basic aggregate design, you may end up creating a class for your aggregate root entity. It must have a globally unique identity and any intrinsic attributes or fields that are necessary for finding the aggregate. You should also add simple and complex behavior. Define every field/attribute/property that is required to identify and find the aggregate, as well as any additional intrinsic fields/attributes/properties that are required for the aggregate to be constructed and left in a valid initial state.
Build testable aggregates. Effectively unit test should test that the aggregate correctly does what it is supposed to do.
Domain Events
A domain event is a record of some business-significant occurrence in a bounded context. It’s a very powerful way to inform interested listeners of important occurrences that have taken place. Some events may be caused by commands, while others may happen due to the detection of some other changing state, such as a date or time.
You generally want to convey the date and time when your domain event occurred. The name should be a statement of a past occurrence and must reflect your model’s ubiquitous language, because it’s vital that you communicate your happenings well. The domain event should hold all the properties that were provided with the command that caused it to be created and it can be enriched with additional data (be careful about how much data). It’s important that the modified aggregate and the domain event are saved together in the same transaction.
Event sourcing can be described as persisting all domain events that have occurred for an aggregate instance as a record of what changed about that aggregate instance. Because the event store is append-only, it makes the storage mechanism extremely fast. It allows its state to be reconstituted from persistence back into memory but there is no need to reconstitute them from storage each time they are used, you can keep your aggregates’ state cached. Another tool is maintaining a snapshot of incremental state of your aggregate.
Event storming technique
Event storming is a rapid design technique that is meant to engage both domain experts and developers. It is very cheap to perform, and it’s a very visual approach. This technique focuses all on events and the business process. At the end, everybody learns something and you all end up with a clear understanding of the model, an unified direction and purpose. You can do this for two hours a day, for three or four days, to gain a deep understanding of your core domain and integrations with your surrounding subdomains. Event Storming is a tool that can be used at any time, not just during project inception. The basic steps are:
Create a series of domain events on sticky notes, with primary focus on the business process. Write the name of each domain event (it should be a verb stated in the past tense) from left to right in the order in which each event occurs in the domain. Sometimes you won’t have a good understanding of the time order, which will probably become obvious, later. Use vertical space to represent parallel processing. Sometimes the outcome of a domain event is a process that needs to run; draw a line with an arrowhead from the domain event to the named process.
Create the command, often an outcome of some user gesture, that cause each domain event (some domain events won’t have a corresponding command). Sometimes, a command can cause multiple domain events or it can cause a process to be run; in this last case, draw a line with an arrowhead from the command to the named process. The process will actually cause one or more commands and subsequent domain events. Command ’s name should be stated in the imperative.
Associate the entity/aggregate (this is a noun) on which the command is executed and that produces the domain event outcome. The command and domain event pairs should adhere to the lower part of the aggregate sticky. Probably, in this step, you may discover new domain events. You may also find that some aggregates are repeatedly used, so create many sticky notes and place them on the timeline.
Draw boundaries and lines with arrows to show flow on your modeling surface. Use solid lines for bounded contexts and dashed lines for subdomains. Then, name your bounded contexts. Draw lines with arrowheads to show the direction of domain events flowing between bounded contexts.
Identify the various views that your users will need to carry out their actions, and important roles for various users. Draw a quick mockup (or wireframe)
