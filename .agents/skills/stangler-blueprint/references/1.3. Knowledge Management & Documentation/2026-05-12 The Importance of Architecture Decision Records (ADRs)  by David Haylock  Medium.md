While this gives us a high-level understanding of the architecture, it often lacks critical details — specifically, why certain decisions were made. Without this context, we find ourselves questioning key aspects of the system: Why was this database technology chosen over another? Why is this service implemented as a microservice instead of a monolith? Why is this API using GraphQL when other parts of the system rely on REST? Without these answers, we are forced to dig through code, ask team members (who might not have been present for the original decisions), or make assumptions that could lead to costly mistakes. This slows down our onboarding, increases the risk of misinterpreting the architecture, and delays feature development. Photo by Alex wong on Unsplash Had Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) been available, they would have provided clear justifications for past choices, allowing us to ramp up quickly and make informed contributions. ADRs also help avoid repeating past discussions or reintroducing discarded approaches. This reinforces the importance of documenting architectural decisions — not just what was built, but why it was built that way. ADRsprovide a structured approach to capturing significant architectural choices, offering context and justification for each decision. When stored inside Git repositories, they become an integral part of the development workflow, fostering better collaboration, transparency, and long-term project sustainability. In this article, we’ll explore the importance of ADRs, their benefits, best practices, and how to implement them effectively. What are Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)? An ADR is a document that captures important architectural decisions made during the lifecycle of a software project. Each ADR typically includes: Title: A short descriptive name for the decision. Context: The problem or situation prompting the decision. Decision: The chosen solution or approach. Consequences: The impact of the decision, including trade-offs and alternatives. Status: Whether the decision is accepted, deprecated, or superseded by another ADR. ADRs act as a living history of architectural evolution, documenting why certain decisions were made and how they align with business and technical requirements. Where to store ADRs? There’s ongoing debate about where to store ADR documents – some suggest platforms like Confluence or internal wikis. However, ADRs should be kept within the context they refer to. The most logical place is the project’s Git repository, as it directly represents the system the ADR pertains to. Why Store ADRs Inside Git Repositories?
Version Control and History Tracking Git provides a powerful mechanism for version control, allowing teams to track changes over time. Storing ADRs inside a Git repository ensures that architectural decisions are versioned alongside the codebase. This allows teams to: Revert to previous decisions if needed. Understand the rationale behind past choices. Maintain a historical record of architectural evolution.
Improved Collaboration and Transparency ADRs encourage team members to discuss and document architectural choices collectively. When stored in a Git repository: Developers, architects, and stakeholders can review and contribute to decisions through pull requests and discussions. New team members can quickly get up to speed on past decisions without needing to rely on oral explanations. Decision-making becomes a structured and inclusive process rather than an ad-hoc, undocumented one.
Seamless Integration with Development Workflow By keeping ADRs in the same Git repository as the codebase: They remain easily accessible to all contributors. They can be referenced in commit messages, pull requests, and issue discussions. Changes to architecture can be reviewed alongside code changes, ensuring alignment.
Maintaining Knowledge Architectural decisions often involve trade-offs that are not immediately apparent. If key decision-makers leave the project, undocumented choices may lead to repeated discussions or, worse, the reimplementation of previously discarded solutions. ADRs mitigate this risk by preserving knowledge within the Git repository.
Ensuring Consistency and Accountability When decisions are explicitly documented, teams can: Hold discussions based on concrete information rather than assumptions. Ensure that architectural guidelines are followed across the project. Maintain consistency in decision-making, especially in large or distributed teams. Best Practices for Implementing ADRs in Git Repositories
Use a Standardised Format Having a consistent structure for ADRs ensures clarity and ease of use. A simple markdown format is widely recommended, as it integrates well with Git and allows for easy diffing and tracking. Store ADRs in a Dedicated Directory A common convention is to keep ADRs in a directory named docs/adr/ or simply adr/ at the root of the Git repository. This keeps them organised and easy to find.
Use a Numbering System Assigning a unique identifier (e.g., ADR-001, ADR-002) to each record helps track decisions in chronological order and simplifies referencing.
Review and Update ADRs Regularly Just like code, architecture decisions evolve. Teams should: Regularly review ADRs to ensure they remain relevant. Mark outdated decisions as deprecated or superseded when necessary. Document revisions and updates transparently.
Leverage Git Features for Discussions and Reviews Use pull requests for proposing new ADRs, allowing team members to discuss and refine decisions before acceptance. Reference ADRs in issue trackers and commit messages to link architectural decisions with implementation details.
Encourage a Culture of Documentation For ADRs to be effective, teams must embrace documentation as a critical part of software development. This requires fostering a culture where: Decision-making is transparent and inclusive. ADRs are written in simple, clear language. Everyone understands the value of maintaining well-documented architecture choices. Conclusion ADRs play a crucial role in maintaining the integrity and sustainability of software projects. By storing ADRs inside Git repositories, teams can ensure that architectural decisions are well-documented, version-controlled, and easily accessible. This practice leads to better collaboration, historical traceability, and informed decision-making, ultimately contributing to the long-term success of a project. By following best practices such as using a standardised format, storing ADRs in a dedicated directory, and leveraging Git’s collaboration features, teams can integrate ADRs seamlessly into their development workflow.
