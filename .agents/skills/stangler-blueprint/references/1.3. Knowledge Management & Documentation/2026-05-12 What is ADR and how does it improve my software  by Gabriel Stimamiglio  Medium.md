The emphasis is on recording not only the outcome, but the entire thought process that led to it. Large technology companies, which operate on a large scale and with high complexity, have refined this definition based on their practical experiences: Spotify defines an ADR as “a document that captures a decision, including the context of how the decision was made and the consequences of adopting it.” This definition highlights the importance of looking forward (consequences) and backward (context). Google Cloud states that an ADR “captures the main options available, the key requirements driving a decision, and the design decisions themselves.” Here, the focus is on comparative analysis and traceability of requirements. Amazon Web Services (AWS) describes it as a document that “describes a choice that the team makes about a significant aspect of the software architecture they plan to build.” AWS emphasizes the significance of the decision, suggesting that not every choice warrants an ADR. Collectively, these definitions paint a clear picture: an ADR is a concise, justified record of an architecturally significant design choice that addresses a specific functional or non-functional requirement. Okay, but why use it? That is the central question. We use ADR to combat a silent but extremely costly problem in software projects: “ architectural amnesia.” You know when a new developer asks why we use technology X instead of Y, and the only answer is “it’s always been that way” or “the old team decided it”? Or when the team spends hours debating the same decision for the third time because no one remembers the original reasoning? That’s exactly where ADR comes in. It serves as your project’s memory, recording not only what was decided, but more importantly, why it was decided. In short, we use ADRs to ensure that critical knowledge about the architecture is not lost over time or when people leave, allowing the team to make future decisions faster, more confidently, and in a more informed manner. Okay, but what are the benefits of using ADRs? ADRs offer a return on investment that goes far beyond simple documentation, impacting the efficiency, culture, and sustainability of a project in the long term. For example, one of the most immediate and frequently cited benefits is the dramatic reduction in the time it takes to integrate new team members. Instead of relying on lengthy explanation sessions with senior engineers, a new developer can read the Architecture Decision Log (ADL) to gain a deep and contextualized understanding of the system. Another important example is cyclical debates. Engineering teams can spend a significant amount of time in cyclical debates on the same issues, a phenomenon known as “relitigation” of decisions. A well-written ADL puts an end to this. By documenting the context, the alternatives considered, the pros and cons of each, and the final rationale, the ADR establishes a baseline for the decision. Note: What is ADL? A single ADR is a snapshot of a decision. The real power of the practice, however, emerges from the collection of all ADRs for a project. This collection is known as the Architecture Decision Log (ADL). How do I know that this decision should become an ADR? This is a crucial question for keeping the process light and efficient. The rule of thumb is: a decision should become an ADR when it is “ architecturally significant.” To find out if this is the case, ask yourself a few key questions: Does the decision have an impact that goes beyond a single team or a single module? Is it difficult or costly to reverse in the future? Does it introduce new technology, a new standard, or a significant external dependency into the project? Does it resolve a technical issue that the team has debated at length and needs to be aligned on? If the answer to any of these questions is “yes,” then that decision is a strong candidate for becoming an ADR. For example, choosing a cloud provider (AWS vs. Google Cloud) deserves an ADR; however, the decision to refactor an internal function within a service probably does not. The goal is to record the choices that shape the long-term structure and constraints of your software. I understand, but how do I implement this in my workflow? Implementing ADRs in your workflow is an agile process that should live alongside your code. The most common and effective way is to treat architectural decisions as code. Start by creating a folder at the root of your repository, such as /docs/adr/. When a significant architectural decision needs to be made (e.g., adopting a new library, defining a communication standard between services), a team member creates a new Markdown file in that folder, using a simple template to describe the context, the decision, and the consequences. This file is then submitted as a Pull Request (PR). The decision is reviewed in the comments of this PR, involving the entire team. Once the proposal is approved, the PR is merged, and the ADR becomes an official and immutable record of the project. This way, you integrate documentation into the development flow that your team already knows, ensuring that it is collaborative, versioned, and always accessible. And how do I write an ADR? To write an Architecture Decision Record (ADR), you should focus on clearly and concisely documenting an architecturally significant decision. Start with a title that summarizes the decision and add a status (such as “Proposed,” “Accepted,” or “Replaced”) and the date. Next, describe the Context section, which should explain the problem, the forces at play, and the constraints that led to the need for a decision. This part is crucial so that future readers, including new team members, can understand the scenario at the time of the decision. The heart of the ADR lies in the following sections. In Options Considered, list the different approaches that were evaluated to solve the problem, briefly detailing the pros and cons of each. This demonstrates that the decision was not made lightly. Next, in the Decision section, clearly state which option was chosen and, crucially, justify “why.” The justification is the most important element, as it explains the logic and factors that led to the final choice. Finally, document the Consequences of the decision, both positive and negative. This helps the team understand the trade-offs and future impacts of the choice, such as new dependencies, changes in the way of working, or possible limitations. Keeping the ADR in a simple format, such as Markdown, and storing it alongside the project’s source code makes it easy for the entire team to access and consult. Conclusion Adopting Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) is a low-cost, high-impact practice for combating the “architectural amnesia” that plagues so many software projects. By documenting not only what was decided, but why and with what consequences, we transform volatile decisions into lasting knowledge for the team. Far from being a bureaucratic burden, the process of creating and maintaining ADRs, especially when integrated into the existing development flow, strengthens communication, accelerates the integration of new members, and ensures that your software evolves in a conscious and sustainable way. Adopting ADRs is, in essence, an investment in the clarity and longevity of your architecture.
