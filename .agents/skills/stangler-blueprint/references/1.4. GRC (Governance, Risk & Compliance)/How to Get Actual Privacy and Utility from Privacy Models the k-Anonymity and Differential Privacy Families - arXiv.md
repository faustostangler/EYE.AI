Abstract
Report issue for preceding element
Privacy models were introduced in the privacy-preserving data publishing and statistical disclosure control literature with the promise to end the need for costly empirical assessment of disclosure risk. We examine how well this promise is kept by the main privacy models. We find they may fail to provide adequate protection guarantees because of problems in their definition or incur unacceptable trade-offs between privacy protection and utility preservation. Specifically, k k -anonymity may not entirely exclude disclosure if enforced with deterministic mechanisms or without constraints on the confidential values. On the other hand, differential privacy (DP) incurs unacceptable utility loss for small budgets and its privacy guarantee becomes meaningless for large budgets. In the latter case, an ex post empirical assessment of disclosure risk becomes necessary, undermining the main appeal of privacy models. Whereas the utility preservation of DP can only be improved by relaxing its privacy guarantees, we argue that a semantic reformulation of k k -anonymity can offer more robust privacy without losing utility with respect to traditional syntactic k k -anonymity.

1 Introduction
Report issue for preceding element
Since the 1960s, research has been conducted with the goal of collecting and publishing analytically useful data that cannot be linked to specific individuals or reveal confidential information about them. Over the years, a substantial body of privacy-enhancing methods has been developed in the literature on statistical disclosure control (SDC) and privacy-preserving data publishing (PPDP).
Report issue for preceding element
The traditional approach to privacy protection in official statistics is heuristic: an SDC method, such as data suppression, sampling, or noise addition, is iteratively tried with varying parameter values until a parameter choice is found that preserves sufficient analytical utility while bringing the disclosure risk below a certain threshold. In this approach, both utility and disclosure risk are evaluated ex post by empirically measuring information loss and the probability of disclosure in actual protected outputs.
Report issue for preceding element
In the late 1990s, due to the surge of the web and the consequent expansion of data collection and exchange beyond official statistics, privacy protection became a mainstream topic in the computer science community, which approached it under a different angle. Specifically, an ex ante privacy condition is stated, which is then enforced on the actual data to be protected by using one or several SDC methods. Since privacy is modeled as the satisfaction of a certain condition, this approach is commonly referred to as privacy model. A significant advantage of privacy models with respect to heuristic SDC methods is that, since the former enforce an ex ante privacy condition, the ex post empirical evaluation of disclosure risk may no longer be necessary. In other words, privacy models are supposed to guarantee a certain level of privacy by design.
Report issue for preceding element
The first privacy model was k k -anonymity [ 16, 15] , which was followed by extensions such as l l -diversity [ 12] and t t -closeness [ 11] . A substantially different family of privacy models was inaugurated by ϵ \epsilon -differential privacy (DP) [ 7] and subsequently expanded by a variety of relaxations [ 14] . This second family is by far the most widespread these days.
Report issue for preceding element
In this paper, we critically review these two families of privacy models and discuss the privacy guarantees they actually provide. In its traditional syntactic formulation, the k k -anonymity family may fail to guarantee privacy in very specific cases. On the other hand, the privacy guarantee of the DP family might be too broad for most applications and become meaningless unless the budget ϵ \epsilon is small, which comes at the cost of unacceptable utility loss. Whereas the preservation of utility by DP can only be improved by relaxing its privacy guarantee, we argue that a semantic reformulation of k k -anonymity can offer more robust guarantees while retaining its favorable and selectable privacy-utility trade-off.
Report issue for preceding element
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Privacy notions, including the types of disclosure risks and the types of data attributes, are reviewed in Section 2. Section 3 gives an overview of privacy models and their privacy promises. Objections and mitigations to k k -anonymity and DP are discussed in Sections 4 and 5, respectively. Conclusions are drawn in Section 6.

2 Privacy notions
Report issue for preceding element
To understand the guarantees offered by privacy models, we first need to consider the types of information disclosure:
Report issue for preceding element
• Identity disclosure occurs when an adversary can identify the subject to whom a released output corresponds. This is known as reidentification. Report issue for preceding element
• Attribute disclosure occurs when seeing the released outputs allows the adversary to determine or closely approximate the value of a subject's confidential attribute. Report issue for preceding element
• Membership disclosure occurs when the adversary can determine whether a certain subject's data were part of the data used to compute the released outputs. Report issue for preceding element
Since revealing confidential attributes ( e.g., income, health condition, etc.) causes a serious privacy threat, attribute disclosure is arguably the most critical type of disclosure. From that point of view, reidentification and membership disclosure only result in disclosure of privacy if they enable attribute disclosure. This will happen when reidentified records ( e.g., patient records) can be unequivocally associated with confidential attributes ( e.g., health conditions), or when inferring membership ( e.g., a patient being a member of a released data set) implicitly reveals confidential values ( e.g., if it is known that all patients in the released data set suffer from the same condition). Moreover, whereas reidentification allows singling out an individual's record in a released data set, membership disclosure becomes irrelevant when the published data are an exhaustive representation of a population ( e.g., a national census) because there is no uncertainty about the membership of any subject.
Report issue for preceding element
Identity and attribute disclosures have been considered in the privacy literature for decades [ 10] , whereas membership disclosure has only gained relevance in the last few years within the machine learning field [ 18] . The reason is that, quite often, the only plausible attack against black-box machine learning models is membership inference.
Report issue for preceding element
In line with the above three types of disclosure, attributes in a data release can be classified into different types. Identifiers are attributes that allow unambiguous reidentification of the individual to whom they refer. Examples include social security numbers or passport numbers. Identifiers should always be suppressed in anonymized data releases. Quasi-identifiers (QI) are attributes that do not lead to re-reidentification when taken one by one, but a combination of them may if i) it is known by the intruder on a target individual and ii) is unique in the population. For example, [ 26] shows that 87% of the population in the United States can be unambiguously identified once their 5-digit ZIP code, birth date, and sex are known. Confidential attributes hold confidential information on the individual ( e.g., salary, health condition, sexual orientation, etc.). As stated above, the primary goal of privacy protection is to prevent intruders from determining or closely approximating confidential information about a specific individual. Non-confidential attributes are those that do not belong to any of the previous categories. As they do not contain confidential information about individuals and cannot be used for record reidentification, they do not need to be taken into account when protecting privacy.

3.1 k k -Anonymity and extensions
Report issue for preceding element
k k -Anonymity [ 16] is a privacy model specifically designed to anonymize outputs consisting of microdata releases, that is, data sets of records with detailed attribute values related to specific individuals.
Report issue for preceding element
k k -Anonymity assumes that the values of the quasi-identifier attributes are known to the intruder, who can use them for reidentification. Anonymized microdata must be computed so that each combination of values of the quasi-identifier attributes is shared by k k or more records, which form a so-called k k -anonymous class. This makes records indistinguishable (and in principle not reidentifiable without ambiguity) within each class. Therefore, the mathematical guarantee that can be given is an upper bound 1 / k 1/k on the risk of reidentification of each individual record.
Report issue for preceding element
k k -Anonymity is not tied to a specific masking mechanism, and can be achieved by masking quasi-identifiers through a variety of SDC methods, including generalization/suppression (the original approach in [ 16] ) or aggregation (the microaggregation approach in [ 6] ).
Report issue for preceding element
Although plain k k -anonymity can protect against reidentification, attribute disclosure may still occur if the values of a confidential attribute within a k k -anonymous class are the same or very similar. In this case, the intruder just needs to find out that their target record belongs to that k k -anonymous class to infer the target's confidential attribute value; that is, attribute disclosure occurs without prior reidentification. To address this issue, k k -anonymity extensions were proposed.
Report issue for preceding element
The first extension was l l -diversity [ 12] , in which anonymized microdata must be calculated so that there are l l “well-represented” values for each confidential attribute within each k k -anonymous class. The meaning of “well-represented” is open to interpretation and can be understood as different values or as values sampled from a distribution whose Shannon entropy is above a certain threshold. Given a criterion of well-representedness, the higher l l , the higher the intruder's uncertainty about the value of any confidential attribute for his/her target record.
Report issue for preceding element
While l l -diversity defines an absolute measure of variability for confidential values, t t -closeness [ 11] is another extension of k k -anonymity whose aim is to limit the gain of information on the confidential attribute in case the intruder manages to locate the k k -anonymous class of their target record. The gain is measured w.r.t. the marginal distribution of the confidential attribute in the entire data set, which is assumed known to the intruder. t t -Closeness requires the anonymized microdata set to be calculated so that the distance between the distribution of each confidential attribute in any k k -anonymous class and the distribution of the attribute in the entire data set is not more than a threshold t t . The smaller t t , the more limited the intruder's gain. A common distance measure to enforce t t -closeness is the earth mover's distance.

3.2 ϵ \epsilon -Differential privacy and relaxations
Report issue for preceding element
ϵ \epsilon -Differential privacy (DP) was originally proposed in [ 7] as a privacy model designed to protect the outputs of interactive queries on an unreleased sensitive data set. The principle underlying DP is that the presence or absence of any single individual's record in the data set should be indistinguishable on the basis of the released query answer.
Report issue for preceding element
More formally, a randomized function κ \kappa gives ϵ \epsilon -differential privacy if, for all neighbor data sets X 1 X_{1} and X 2 X_{2} that differ in one record and all S ⊂ R  a  n  g  e  ( κ ) S\subset Range(\kappa) , we have
Report issue for preceding element
Pr  ( κ  ( X 1 ) ∈ S ) ≤ exp  ( ϵ ) × Pr  ( κ  ( X 2 ) ∈ S ) . \Pr(\kappa(X_{1})\in S)\leq\exp(\epsilon)\times\Pr(\kappa(X_{2})\in S).
The exponential parameter ϵ \epsilon (also called privacy budget) defines how indistinguishable the presence or absence of any single record is based on the query answers: the smaller ϵ \epsilon , the higher the protection. Hence, the anonymized query output must be computed using randomization, and the mathematical guarantee offered by DP is an upper bound in terms of the parameter ϵ \epsilon on the risk of membership disclosure for any individual who contributed to the unreleased original data set.
Report issue for preceding element
DP is commonly enforced by adding Laplacian noise that is inversely proportional to ϵ \epsilon and directly proportional to the global sensitivity of the query, that is, how much the output of the (unrandomized) query can change due to the addition, deletion, or change of a single record in the data set. Mild noise may suffice for aggregated statistical queries, such as the mean, but much larger noise is needed for more sensitive queries, like maximum, minimum, or identity queries ( i.e., querying the value of the i i -th record).
Report issue for preceding element
Even if DP was designed to protect interactive queries, it has been extensively applied to non-interactive scenarios such as continuous data collection and (micro)data releases [ 2, 28] . However, applying DP to record-level data (as is the case for data collection or microdata release) is equivalent to answering identity queries that, because of the large sensitivity of any individual's data, require adding a large amount of noise to attain a “safe” ϵ \epsilon . This severely hampers the utility of the DP-protected outputs [ 21] .
Report issue for preceding element
Due to the impossibility of reconciling data utility with small enough ϵ \epsilon in non-interactive settings, a variety of relaxations of the DP definition have been proposed. The most widely used are:
Report issue for preceding element
• ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -DP [ 7] , where parameter δ \delta is the probability that pure DP is not satisfied. Report issue for preceding element
• Rényi DP (RDP) [ 13] is a relaxation of DP based on the Rényi divergence through parameter α ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) \alpha\in(1,\infty) , providing a quantifiable measure of privacy leakage. When α = 1 \alpha=1 , RDP converges to the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and, for α = ∞ \alpha=\infty , it converges to pure DP. Report issue for preceding element
• Zero-Concentrated DP (zCDP) [ 3] is also defined in terms of the Rényi divergence. However, a single parameter ρ ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) \rho\in(1,\infty) restricts parameter α \alpha . For ρ = 1 \rho=1 , zCDP is equivalent to the KL divergence and, for ρ = ∞ \rho=\infty , it approaches the max-divergence and pure DP. Report issue for preceding element
4 Objections and mitigations to k k -anonymity
Report issue for preceding element
k k -Anonymity is designed to protect against identity disclosure and, unlike DP, offers a clear and meaningful guarantee for any value of its parameter. Specifically, for any k > 1 k>1 , the probability of successful reidentification should be at most 1 / k 1/k , which should eliminate the need to empirically evaluate the risk of reidentification ex post for any value of k k .
Report issue for preceding element
However, k k -anonymity is often dismissed and considered to be more obsolete than DP due to alleged flaws, which we will discuss next. For each of them, we highlight solutions or mitigations.
Report issue for preceding element
4.1 Formal problems of the k k -anonymity definition
Report issue for preceding element
The need to make assumptions on the list of attributes that can be used as quasi-identifiers by an intruder has often been pointed out as a weakness of k k -anonymity. A way to circumvent the need for such assumptions is to consider all attributes as potential quasi-identifiers. Obviously, the more attributes are masked to enforce k k -anonymity, the less utility is preserved by the anonymized microdata set. However, masking all attributes is exactly what DP also does when used to generate anonymized microdata. In this context, k k -anonymity is more flexible and consistent than DP, for several reasons:
Report issue for preceding element
Better utility can be attained with k k -anonymity if one can make realistic assumptions on the attributes that can or cannot be plausibly used by an intruder as quasi-identifiers. Report issue for preceding element
For k k -anonymity to be fulfilled, it is enough to mask quasi-identifier values only in those original records whose quasi-identifier combination has frequency less than k k , rather than masking all attribute values in all records as DP does. Report issue for preceding element
k k -Anonymity ensures that all original records at risk ( i.e., with reidentification risk above 1 / k 1/k ) are modified for any value of k k ; in contrast, DP with large ϵ \epsilon may leave many records at risk virtually untouched or with extremely small noise. Report issue for preceding element
A more recent criticism stems from the syntactic formulation of k k -anonymity, which focuses on the structure of the data, rather than on their meaning. Indeed, the requirement of having at least k k records sharing the same combination of quasi-identifiers can be achieved with deterministic mechanisms that are vulnerable to attack. Specifically, a data set can be made k k -anonymous by replacing attribute values of non- k k -anonymous records by common generalizations ( e.g., { U  S  A , F  r  a  n  c  e } → { C  o  u  n  t  r  y } {USA,France}\rightarrow{Country} ). However, when generalizations fulfill minimality, which means that no record is generalized more than necessary to achieve the k k -anonymity property, and the hierarchy of generalizations is known, a recent study [ 5] shows that it might be possible to downcode ( i.e., undo) some generalizations because the minimal generalizations of specific data distributions are deterministic and (partially) reversible.
Report issue for preceding element
Although [ 5] hints that this vulnerability affects all approaches to k k -anonymity, in reality it only applies to k k -anonymity via minimum generalization, which is very rarely used. Actually, any mechanism to achieve k k -anonymity that is not reversible can withstand downcoding:
Report issue for preceding element
• If generalization is used, although minimum generalization is the least utility-damaging, it is computationally hard to find it. In practice, k k -anonymity via generalization is achieved via suboptimal heuristics that yield non-minimum generalizations –that are not reversible–. Additionally, non-minimum generalization is often combined with suppression to limit information loss, and suppression is not reversible [ 15, 27] . Report issue for preceding element
• Averaging the quasi-identifier values within each k k -anonymous class is another usual mechanism for k k -anonymity that is not reversible [ 6] . Report issue for preceding element
In any case, it is true that the syntactic formulation of k k -anonymity does not exclude the use of a mechanism such as minimum generalization that is vulnerable to downcoding attacks. To address this issue, we suggest employing a semantic formulation that focuses on the meaning of the data, rather than on their structure. Specifically, probabilistic k k -anonymity eliminates the standard k k -anonymity requirement that any combination of quasi-identifiers be replicated k k or more times and makes the privacy guarantee explicit, as follows [ 19] .
Report issue for preceding element
A data set 𝒟 ′ \mathcal{D}^{\prime} generated from an original data set 𝒟 \mathcal{D} via mechanism 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is said to satisfy probabilistic k k -anonymity if, for any non-anonymous external data set ℰ \mathcal{E} , the probability that an intruder knowing 𝒟 ′ \mathcal{D}^{\prime} , 𝒜 \mathcal{A} and ℰ \mathcal{E} correctly links any record in ℰ \mathcal{E} with its corresponding record (if any) in 𝒟 ′ \mathcal{D}^{\prime} is at most 1 / k 1/k .
Report issue for preceding element
Since probabilistic k k -anonymity drops the k k -fold replication requirement, it can be enforced through a broader range of mechanisms than k k -anonymity. These include traditional methods used for k k -anonymity (generalization, suppression) as well as permutation-based approaches ( e.g., clustering records in groups of at least k k based on quasi-identifiers followed by random permutation of quasi-identifier values within each group [ 19] , or the Anatomy mechanism [ 25] ). While generalization and suppression produce truthful but less detailed data, permutation-based methods yield perturbed data, but perfectly preserve the marginal distribution of every quasi-identifier attribute and, hence, its univariate statistics, such as means and variances. This can be advantageous for certain secondary analyses. Due to their randomness, permutation-based methods are also not reversible.
Report issue for preceding element
On the other hand, since probabilistic k k -anonymity is defined directly in terms of its privacy guarantee (a maximum reidentification probability of 1 / k 1/k for any record), any (partially) reversible method –such as the above-mentioned minimum generalization attacked in [ 5] – would violate the definition. This makes the probabilistic k k -anonymity model inherently robust against downcoding attacks while keeping intact the guarantee intended by k k -anonymity.
Report issue for preceding element
4.2 Attribute disclosure
Report issue for preceding element
A more obvious and dangerous weakness of k k -anonymity is its inability to prevent attribute disclosure, as introduced in Section 3.1. However, this can be fixed by applying l l -diversity or t t -closeness on top of k k -anonymity.
Report issue for preceding element
Like in the case of k k -anonymity, l l -diversity offers a meaningful guarantee for any value of its parameter: for any l > 1 l>1 , unequivocal inferences of the confidential attribute value are no longer possible, and if a set of l − 1 l-1 different values is augmented with an l l -th different value, the intruder's uncertainty increases. This provides an ex ante guarantee that is meaningful and holds for any value of l l . However, due to the general formulation of l l -diversity, which does not exactly prescribe the way to select the l l “well-represented” values, one should carefully consider the following issues [ 11] :
Report issue for preceding element
• Skewness. When the distribution of the confidential attribute in the overall data set is strongly skewed, satisfying l l -diversity may be counterproductive as far as attribute disclosure is concerned. Consider the case of a medical data set in which the confidential attribute records the presence or absence of a given disease. Assume that 99% of the individuals are negative. Releasing a k k -anonymous class with 50% positives and 50% negatives is optimal in terms of l l -diversity but is, indeed, potentially disclosive. After the release of such data, each of the individuals in the k k -anonymous class is found to have a 50% probability of being positive in the listed disease, while the prior probability (before the release) of being positive was only 1%. Report issue for preceding element
• Similarity. Given l ′ > l l^{\prime}>l , l ′ l^{\prime} -diversity may be more disclosive than l l -diversity if the semantic closeness of the l ′ l^{\prime} values is higher than that of the l l values. For example, a k k -anonymous class containing l ′ = 4 l^{\prime}=4 very similar income values involves more attribute disclosure than a class with l = 3 l=3 more spread income values. Report issue for preceding element
t t -Closeness also protects against attribute disclosure, but unlike l l -diversity, its guarantee becomes less and less meaningful as the value of t t increases. This loss of meaning for large parameter values is reminiscent of DP. In fact, t t -closeness and DP are related: in [ 20] it is shown that the information that an intruder obtains from accessing an exp  ( ϵ ) \exp(\epsilon) -close microdata set satisfies ϵ \epsilon -DP for the confidential attribute. Indeed, DP guarantees that the knowledge gain obtained from the response to a query on an individual's confidential attribute is at most exp  ( ϵ ) \exp(\epsilon) ; that is, the distribution of the response must differ at most by a factor of exp  ( ϵ ) \exp(\epsilon) from the assumed prior knowledge, which is the marginal distribution of the confidential attribute. Therefore, just as the ϵ \epsilon -DP guarantee becomes meaningless for large values of ϵ \epsilon , so does t t -closeness for large t t .
Report issue for preceding element
Moreover, t t -closeness assumes that the marginal distributions of the confidential attributes are known. This makes sense when a marginal distribution is not related to a specific individual. However, when one of the individuals in the data set is known to have an outlying value ( e.g., in a given economic sector the largest firm in the sector could be easily identifiable from the value of the confidential attribute ”Turnover”), the marginal distribution is itself disclosive. Also, if the entire data set shares the same (unknown) confidential value ( e.g., the data set is a sample of patients that are positive of a certain disease), even releasing a 0-close version will disclose the common value of the confidential attribute.
Report issue for preceding element
Both l l -diversity and t t -closeness can be enforced on top of k k -anonymity –or, preferably, probabilistic k k -anonymity– by taking into account their constraints on the confidential attributes when forming the k k -anonymous classes [ 23] .
Report issue for preceding element
4.3 Postprocessing immunity and composability
Report issue for preceding element
Robustness against postprocessing requires that further processing of the protected output (without access to the original data) should still fulfill the privacy model. Since standard k k -anonymity requires that each combination of values of the quasi-identifier attributes be shared by k k or more records, any postprocessing that breaks this rule, such as sampling or modifying individual records, makes the output no longer k k -anonymous.
Report issue for preceding element
The clash with postprocessing stems from the syntactic definition of standard k k -anonymity, according to which the quasi-identifier values of any record must be replicated at least k k times in the k k -anonymous data set. However, postprocessing does not affect the probability of reidentification, since it is performed without access to the original data. Therefore, if one follows the suggestion above to use a semantic definition based on the risk of reidentification, postprocessing immunity is satisfied. In particular, probabilistic k k -anonymity satisfies postprocessing immunity.
Report issue for preceding element
Regarding composition, even though k k -anonymity fulfills parallel composition (merging two k k -anonymous disjoint data sets yields a k k -anonymous data set), it does not provide a deterministic and smooth degradation of its privacy guarantee under sequential composition: merging two non-disjoint k k -anonymous data sets may rapidly result in a 1-anonymous ( i.e., unprotected) data set. Take a target record belonging to the intersection of the two data sets and consider the intersection of the two k k -anonymous classes that contain the record (one from the first data set and the other from the second data set). If the two k k -anonymous classes are different, their intersection contains fewer than k k records but is known to contain the target record; hence, k k -anonymity is broken. A specific consequence is that k k -anonymity is not suitable for scenarios that involve incremental data releases on the same subjects.
Report issue for preceding element
5 Objections and mitigations to ϵ \epsilon -differential privacy
Report issue for preceding element
The DP guarantee focuses mainly on protecting against membership disclosure. In fact, the expression ( 3.2) refers to the intruder's ability to infer membership, that is, the presence or absence of an individual's record in the data set. Protection against identity disclosure and attribute disclosure are obtained as consequences:
Report issue for preceding element
• To make membership indistinguishable, all DP outputs must be perturbed so much that the values of attributes of original records remain protected; in other words, the influence of the value of a particular attribute in a particular record on the DP outputs is negligible. This protects against attribute disclosure. Report issue for preceding element
• If the presence or absence of any record cannot be established and the record's attribute values cannot be estimated from the DP outputs, the attacker cannot mount a reidentification attack: reidentification needs a record that can be attributed to someone. Report issue for preceding element
This threefold guarantee is more robust than that of k k -anonymity, but it often comes at the cost of unacceptable privacy-utility trade-offs, as we discuss below.
Report issue for preceding element
5.1 DP guarantees do not hold for large ϵ \epsilon
Report issue for preceding element
DP guarantees perfect indistinguishability only for ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 . More specifically, in this case, DP achieves Shannon's perfect secrecy [ 17] , regarding membership, identity, and attribute disclosures:
Report issue for preceding element
• Perfect secrecy for membership and identity. Let T T be a random variable that takes the value 1 if the target individual's record is a member of the original data set and the value 0 otherwise. Let Y Y be the query output randomized with function κ \kappa and ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 . Before seeing Y Y , the intruder's uncertainty on T T is H  ( T ) H(T) , where H H stands for Shannon's entropy. After seeing Y Y , the intruder's uncertainty on T T is H  ( T | Y ) H(T|Y) . Since expression ( 3.2) holds with equality for ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 , Y Y tells nothing about the membership of any single record in the original data set, that is, H  ( T | Y ) = H  ( T ) H(T|Y)=H(T) , which is Shannon's perfect secrecy condition. Preventing membership inference effectively mitigates identity disclosure because reidentification presupposes membership (only members can be reidentified). Report issue for preceding element
• Perfect secrecy for attribute values. If we assume the value R R of the target record to be unknown to the intruder, the intruder's uncertainty on R R is H  ( R ) H(R) before seeing the query answer Y Y . After seeing Y Y , the intruder's uncertainty is H  ( R | Y ) H(R|Y) . The downside of perfect secrecy is that the query answers have no connection whatsoever to the underlying original data, that is, the answers are completely random or, in other words, analytically useless. In practice, the inventors of DP also consider small ϵ \epsilon values ( i.e., ϵ ≤ 1 \epsilon\leq 1 ) “safe” [ 8] .

Unfortunately, replacing ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 with a small positive budget ϵ \epsilon is not free from issues:
Report issue for preceding element
• On the utility side, a small budget ϵ \epsilon is still incompatible with utility-preserving data releases, which are the use cases in most demand [ 28] . Report issue for preceding element
• On the privacy side, a small positive ϵ \epsilon no longer ensures perfect indistinguishability or perfect protection against disclosure of membership, identity, or confidential attributes. Strictly speaking, the formal indistinguishability guarantee of DP fades away as ϵ \epsilon grows. Whatever protection is achieved under ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 results from the noise added to satisfy DP. Therefore, protection against membership, identity, and attribute disclosure must be empirically verified ex post. This is a major shortcoming because the main attractive of using a privacy model is to dispense with such an empirical check. Report issue for preceding element
In this respect, many research works and most practical applications have employed unreasonably large ϵ \epsilon (sometimes even more than 50 [ 4] ) to keep their data useful enough [ 2, 28] . Although DP does not provide any real privacy guarantee in such loose implementations, most of them confidently state that their data are “DP-protected” and do not feel the need to experimentally evaluate ex post the actual privacy achieved [ 28] .

In an attempt to mitigate these issues and reach a better utility-privacy trade-off, one may resort to DP relaxations. When introducing DP above, we mentioned the most common DP relaxations, which allow smaller ϵ \epsilon values to be achieved with less added noise, thereby improving utility preservation. However, this comes at the cost of a higher probability of violating the pure DP guarantee, which adds further uncertainty about whether the ex ante guarantees are satisfied. For example, if ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -DP is used, there is a δ \delta probability that pure ϵ \epsilon -DP is not satisfied. Other relaxations of DP such as RDP or zCDP generally leak more private information than ϵ \epsilon -DP, and approach ϵ \epsilon -DP only asymptotically. Since these relaxations provide less privacy than pure ϵ \epsilon -DP, and the latter does not achieve perfect indistinguishability for ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , the ex ante guarantees offered by such relaxations are hardly sufficient to avoid the need for ex post verification.

More interesting are the variants that deviate from DP in a deterministic way. Metric DP [ 1] is in fact a generalization of DP that makes the guarantee of indistiguishability between two records x x and x ′ x^{\prime} not only inversely proportional to ϵ \epsilon (as pure DP does), but also inversely proportional to the distance d  ( x , x ′ ) d(x,x^{\prime}) between them. Closer records are more indistinguishable from the metric DP output than more distant records. The intuition is that ϵ \epsilon of DP is replaced by ϵ  d  ( x , x  ' ) \epsilon d(x,x`) in metric DP. Another option is individual DP [ 22] , which requires indistinguishability only between records of the original data set and its corresponding neighbor data sets, instead of across any pair of neighbor data sets. As a result, it protects individuals with the same privacy guarantees as standard DP against identity/attribute disclosure, although the DP guarantees against membership disclosure are not preserved for groups of individuals. Both variants are deterministic relaxations of the DP guarantee that enable improved utility preservation for a given ϵ \epsilon –or, conversely, comparable utility under smaller (stronger) ϵ \epsilon – than pure DP. In fact, both have been designed to better cope with the needs of non-interactive data releases.

5.2 Protecting membership incurs excessive perturbation and might be unnecessary
Report issue for preceding element
As stated above, the DP definition specifically targets membership disclosure. However, since membership is a very general feature that concerns all records in a data set, DP with small or even moderate ϵ \epsilon requires adding random noise to most if not all records, regardless of how vulnerable they are to identity and/or attribute disclosure. This means that records with very common values, which are inherently protected against (identity and attribute) disclosure, get the same amount of noise as rarer records with more outlying values. The consequence is a severe degradation in the utility of DP outputs, even though protection against membership inference is very often unnecessary, for example, when it does not implicitly entail attribute disclosure or when membership is already known –as in cases where the data set exhaustively represents a population–.
Report issue for preceding element
As in the previous section, one mitigation is to adopt alternative DP definitions that relax protection against membership disclosure. For example, individual DP forgoes membership disclosure protection while preserving guarantees for individuals, allowing for significantly better utility when handling attributes with large or unbounded domains [ 24] .

5.3 Post-processing immunity and composability

Post-processing immunity and composability are two widely acknowledged advantages of DP. However, there are some nuances to consider when sequential composition is involved.

If mechanisms 𝒜 1 \mathcal{A}{1} and 𝒜 2 \mathcal{A}{2} satisfy ϵ 1 \epsilon_{1} -DP and ϵ 2 \epsilon_{2} -DP, respectively, their combined application on non-disjoint data satisfies ( ϵ 1 + ϵ 2 )  -DP (\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2})\text{-DP} . Although this looks like a smooth degradation of the privacy guarantees, we should realize that:

• The indistinguishability guarantee of decreases exponentially with ϵ \epsilon . Therefore, adding two budgets ϵ 1 + ϵ 2 \epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2} involves an exponential degradation in privacy with respect to the situation prior to sequential composition. Report issue for preceding element
• Under sequential composition –which applies to all attributes and non-independent records in data releases, or to successive data releases or data collection on the same individuals–, the effective ϵ \epsilon grows quickly. This issue has been observed in many practical applications, where sequential composition led to two- or even three-digit values of ϵ \epsilon , despite initially setting seemingly 'safe' values of ϵ ≤ 1 \epsilon\leq 1 [ 28] . Thus, while the sequential composition of DP offers a straightforward way to quantify the effective ϵ \epsilon when dealing with non-disjoint data, it does not solve the challenge of ensuring meaningful privacy in scenarios such as successive data releases or continuous data collection.

6 Conclusions
Report issue for preceding element
Privacy models are an attractive option for data anonymization, because they promise to free the data protector from the need to perform costly, tedious, context-specific, and error-prone ex post assessments of the extant risk of disclosure. In this paper, we have explored to what extent this promise is upheld by the two main families of privacy models, namely the k k -anonymity family and the differential privacy family. Our findings are that both families face objections that must be mitigated for their ex ante guarantees to be effective. This contradicts the common belief that k k -anonymity is more obsolete than DP and too limited for practical use, while all that is necessary to protect privacy is to enforce DP and “never look back” [ 9] : Both families have their own shortcomings, but also complementary strengths.
Report issue for preceding element
The syntactic definition of k k -anonymity poses formal problems because, when enforced through minimum generalization, it may allow recovering the original data from the k k -anonymous data. On the other hand, it does not satisfy postprocessing immunity. However, taking a semantic definition of k k -anonymity focused on limiting re-identification risk to 1 / k 1/k is a sound solution that fixes both issues. As for the well-known lack of protection against attribute disclosure, it is meaningfully fixed by extensions such as l l -diversity or t t -closeness. Overall, k k anonymity provides a robust solution to protect data releases and, through its targeted masking, can achieve a more favorable privacy–utility trade-off compared to DP.
Report issue for preceding element
In the case of DP, strict indistinguishability is satisfied only for ϵ = 0 \epsilon=0 . For positive ϵ \epsilon , indistinguishability is not perfect. Furthermore, small positive ϵ \epsilon values are likely to result in an unacceptable loss of utility, whereas larger ϵ \epsilon values offer an indistinguishability guarantee so degraded that it is mostly void. This is mitigated through the use of relaxations, which have been widely adopted in practical applications [ 28] . However, those relaxations that entail a probability of the DP condition not holding further degrade the ex ante guarantee and can hardly relieve the data protector of the need to conduct an ex post evaluation of the disclosure risk. This basically cancels the attractiveness of using DP as a privacy model. Variants of DP that relax the guarantee in a deterministic way are more reassuring, while still providing significant utility gains. Anyway, selecting an appropriate DP relaxation –among the dozens proposed in the literature [ 14] – remains a major challenge for practitioners, as it must balance meaningful parameterization with task-specific utility preservation. Nevertheless, DP is better suited to the interactive applications for which it was originally designed –applications that, notably, have been the only ones to successfully reconcile reasonable utility with meaningful privacy parameters in practice [ 28] .
Report issue for preceding element
k k -Anonymity does not offer composability when applied to several overlapping data sets. In contrast, DP offers attractive composition properties, although indistinguishability exponentially weakens at each sequential composition. Admittedly, no other privacy model in the literature performs better than DP in the case of sequential composition. This suggests that current privacy models are not very well suited for protecting incremental data releases or data streams. It is an open problem to find privacy models whose privacy guarantee is meaningful and degrades more gracefully after sequential composition [ 28] .
