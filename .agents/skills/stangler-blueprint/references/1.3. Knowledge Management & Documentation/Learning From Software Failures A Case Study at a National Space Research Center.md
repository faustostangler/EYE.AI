Report issue for preceding element Software failures can have significant consequences, making learning from failures a critical aspect of software engineering. While software organizations are recommended to conduct postmortems to learn from failures, the effectiveness and adoption of these practices vary widely. Understanding how engineers gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures is essential for improving software reliability and preventing recurring failures. High-reliability organizations (HROs) often develop software systems where failures carry catastrophic risks, requiring continuous learning practices to ensure reliability. These organizations provide a valuable setting to examine practices and challenges for learning from software failures. Such insight could help develop processes and tools to improve reliability and to prevent recurring failures. However, we lack in-depth industry perspectives on the practices and challenges associated with failure analysis and learning. Report issue for preceding element To address this gap, we conducted a case study through 10 in-depth interviews with research software engineers at a national space research center. We examine how they learn from failures: how they gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures. To assess the transferability of our findings, we include data from 5 additional interviews at other HROs. Our findings provide insight on how software engineers learn from failures in practice. To summarize our findings: (1) failure learning is informal, ad-hoc, and inconsistently integrated into SDLC; (2) recurring failures persist due to the absence of structured processes; and (3) key challenges, including time constraints, knowledge loss due to team turnover & fragmented documentation, and weak process enforcement, undermine efforts to systematically learn from failures. Our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of how software engineers learn from failures and offer guidance for improving failure management practices. Introduction Report issue for preceding element A core engineering principle is the systematic analysis of failures to prevent their recurrence (Petroski, 1994; Dalcher, 1994) . This approach has been effective across various engineering disciplines, contributing to the low failure rates of e.g., buildings, aircraft, and nuclear reactors. A key method for institutionalizing learning from software failures is the use of postmortem and retrospective practices, where engineers systematically analyze failures and gather lessons learned (Collier et al., 1996) . However, despite the recognized importance of these practices, they are rarely followed consistently as part of the software engineering process within organizations (Vieira et al., 2019; Kasi et al., 2008; Dingsøyr, 2005; Glass, 2002; Keegan and Turner, 2001) . We believe that one contributing factor to the persistent high rate of software failures is the limited adoption and effectiveness of practices to learn from failures (Anandayuvaraj and Davis, 2023) . Although existing works have provided general methods for conducting postmortems (Dingsøyr, 2005; Tiedeman, 1990; Collier et al., 1996; Myllyaho et al., 2004; Stålhane et al., 2003; Dingsøyr et al., 2001) and observations on how postmortem practices have been implemented (Desouza et al., 2005; Washburn Jr et al., 2016; Birk et al., 2002; Kasi et al., 2008; Lyytinen and Robey, 1999; Dingsøyr et al., 2007; Vieira et al., 2019) , we lack empirical insight on the practices and challenges faced by engineers to learn from failures: how they gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures.. Report issue for preceding element Such insights might be particularly valuable to study in high-reliability organizations (HROs), such as those in aerospace, healthcare, and finance, because their software systems operate under strict safety, security, and performance constraints to maintain reliability (Christianson et al., 2011) . In these environments, software failures can have severe consequences, as demonstrated by recent software incidents with catastrophic impacts (Anandayuvaraj et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2014; Wong et al., 2017, 2009; Zollers et al., 2005; National Research Council, 2007) , ranging from financial losses to threats to human life. As highlighted by the National Academy of Engineers (National Research Council, 2007) , software engineering failures are widespread, with annual costs estimated in the trillions of dollars (Krigsman, 2009) , emphasizing the need to reduce software failures. Consequently, organizations should invest resources into software failure analysis and learning processes to reduce recurrence and improve reliability. Report issue for preceding element Our work presents a case study to examine how engineers at an HRO learn from failures. We conducted 10 semi-structured interviews with software engineering researchers at a national space research center. To assess the transferability of our findings, we include 5 additional interviews with engineers from four other HROs. The interviews focused on participants' practices and challenges in gathering, documenting, sharing, and applying lessons learned for ongoing improvement. Report issue for preceding element Our results provide insight into how software engineers in HROs learn from failures. Although HROs are expected to excel at failure management, our study reveals that structured processes for learning from failures is often informal and ad hoc. Systemic barriers, such as time constraints, documentation gaps, and team turnover, limit effective learning. As a result, recurring failures persist despite general process adherence. In summary, we make the following contributions: Report issue for preceding element (1) We characterize the practices used by engineers in an HRO to gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures. Report issue for preceding element (2) We identify key challenges — technical, organizational, and cultural — that impact learning from failures. Report issue for preceding element (3) We propose recommendations and research directions to enable learning from failures. Report issue for preceding element Significance: Our study provides insights on the ( ad hoc) practices currently used in some HROs to learn from failures. Our study highlights organizational, technical, and cultural obstacles to more effective failure management. We also provide recommendations for practitioners to institutionalize structured practices to learn from failures. We hope these insights facilitate continuous improvement and learning, with the goal of reducing recurring failures in high-reliability engineering contexts. Report issue for preceding element
Background and Related Work Report issue for preceding element We discuss the phenomenon of learning from failures in traditional engineering disciplines ( section 2.1) and in software engineering ( section 2.2). Report issue for preceding element Definition: Learning from Failures Learning from failures refers to practices by which practitioners gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures (Dingsøyr, 2005; Kasi et al., 2008; Christianson et al., 2011) . Report issue for preceding element 2.1. Learning from failures in engineering Report issue for preceding element An essential principle of engineering is to analyze failures, learn from them, and implement mitigation to reduce the risk of recurrence in the future (Petroski, 1994; Reason, 1990; Dalcher, 1994) . This principle has been successful in many engineering disciplines, where lessons from failures has contributed to the low failure rates of, e.g., medical devices, aircraft, railway systems, and nuclear reactors. For example, there are databases of failures in medical devices (Commissioner, 2022) , aviation (ASR, [n. d.]) , aerospace (NASA, 2023) , railways (Rai, 2017) , and chemicals (Inv, [n. d.]) , to enable practitioners in those fields to learn from past failures. Methodologies like Six Sigma are used by manufacturing (Gijo et al., 2011) , chemical (Falcón et al., 2012) , civil (Morales et al., 2016) , and automotive (Antony et al., 2005) engineering fields to systematically analyze errors and improve processes (Antony, 2002) . Report issue for preceding element Non-software engineering disciplines emphasize learning from past failures to identify faults and mitigate them in earlier development stages ( e.g., FMEA (Goddard, 2000) ), because the cost of addressing faults post-implementation can be significant (Zage and Zage, 2003; McConnell, 2001; Stecklein et al., 2004) . The physical constraints of traditional engineering fields often lead to Waterfall-type process. Repairing errors in such processes is costly (Royce, 1970) . Report issue for preceding element 2.2. Report issue for preceding element Across all schools of software engineering thought, from ISO (International Organization for Standardization, 2018; ISO, 2015) to Agile (Cockburn, 2002; Derby et al., 2006; Ken Schwaber, 2020) and beyond (standards (IEE, 2014) , frameworks (Beck, 2000; Collier et al., 1996; Fagan, 1977, 1999) , and books (Gilb and Graham, 1993; Beyer et al., 2016) , etc.), guidelines agree that software engineers should also analyze failures and learn from them to improve their processes, as illustrated in Figure 1. Software organizations employ postmortem methods (Collier et al., 1996) to help teams reflect on their development experiences and problems to improve future development practices (Dingsøyr, 2005) . The goal of postmortems is to decrease the likelihood of future failures, by identifying lessons learned from reflecting on past failures and successes. Report issue for preceding element Existing literature on software postmortems can be broadly categorized into two areas: methods for conducting postmortems and experiences conducting them. First, researchers have presented different methods to conduct postmortems (Dingsøyr, 2005; Tiedeman, 1990; Collier et al., 1996; Myllyaho et al., 2004; Stålhane et al., 2003; Dingsøyr et al., 2001) . These works have outlined approaches for identifying root causes, facilitating discussions, and gathering actionable insights. Additionally, there is grey literature on postmortems, from software philosophies ( e.g., DevOps (Kim et al., 2021) ), methodologies ( e.g., the agile principles (Derby et al., 2006) and Scrum (Ken Schwaber, 2020) ), standards ( e.g., ISO/IEC 90003 (International Organization for Standardization, 2018) ), and other guidelines ( e.g., Google's SRE handbook (Beyer et al., 2016) and the NASA Procedural Requirements (NAS, [n. d.]a) ). Second, researchers have also shared engineers' experiences and the perceived challenges in conducting postmortems (Desouza et al., 2005; Dingsøyr et al., 2007; Kasi et al., 2008; Washburn et al., 2016; Birk et al., 2002; Vieira et al., 2019; Gibek et al., 2024; Sillito and Pope, 2024b) . These studies highlight issues such as time and resource constraints (Desouza et al., 2005; Kasi et al., 2008; Vieira et al., 2019) , difficulties in capturing and retaining lessons learned (Lyytinen and Robey, 1999; Kasi et al., 2008; Vieira et al., 2019) , and cultural barriers that discourage open discussion of failures (Desouza et al., 2005; Kasi et al., 2008; Vieira et al., 2019) . However, neither body of work fully addresses the gap in understanding how engineers actually learn from failures in practice and the obstacles they face throughout this process. Report issue for preceding element As a starting point, a few organizations have shared practices and experience reports. Practices are often structured around (1) gathering, (2) documenting, (3) sharing, and (4) applying lessons from failures (Kasi et al., 2008) , as illustrated in Figure 1. Structured reviews or retrospectives are recommended to (1) gather lessons from technical and socio-organizational factors that contributed to failures (Dingsøyr, 2005; Beyer et al., 2016; NAS, [n. d.]a) . For example, Google describes a blameless postmortem approach to foster a culture of learning (Beyer et al., 2016) . Documentation practices are recommended to (2) document not only root causes but also broader context, decision rationale, mitigation efforts, and lessons in an accessible and durable format (Dingsøyr, 2005; Beyer et al., 2016; NAS, [n. d.]b, [n. d.]a; Johnson, 2000) . For example, organizations like NASA (NAS, [n. d.]b) and ESA (Les, 2024) maintain databases of lessons learned. (3) Sharing often relies on both formal mechanisms like on-boarding training, cross-team reviews, and newsletters, as well as informal mechanisms like mentoring and reading clubs (Dingsøyr, 2005; Beyer et al., 2016) . However, literature on (4) applying lessons learned is limited and their long-term effectiveness remains uncertain (Pope and Sillito, 2024; Sillito and Pope, 2024b) . A suggested practice is to create action items from lessons (Dingsøyr, 2005; Beyer et al., 2016) . Report issue for preceding element However, many other organizations lack processes to study and learn from failures (Vieira et al., 2019; Kasi et al., 2008; Dingsøyr, 2005; Glass, 2002; Keegan and Turner, 2001) . In addition, there is skepticism about how carefully these processes are followed in organizations that have them (Kasi et al., 2008; Sillito and Pope, 2024a) . The aforementioned exemplar organizations publish their policies but rarely share engineers' experiences in following them, nor whether it leads to sustained learning, especially beyond the postmortem room into the rest of the engineering process. To address this gap, we turn to HROs, which are theoretically expected to exemplify continuous learning and failure management (Christianson et al., 2011) . Report issue for preceding element
Reseach Questions Report issue for preceding element In our analysis of the literature discussed in section 2, we observed that there is little empirical data on how software engineers learn from software failures: how they gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures. We lack in-depth qualitative descriptions of industry experiences, creating a gap in our understanding of practices to learn from failures. We do not know which how far practice is from theory, which practices are effective, nor the challenges in this engineering process. Report issue for preceding element 4.1. Research design and rationale Report issue for preceding element Our research questions require insight into practitioner perspectives on the learning-from-failures process. In choosing a methodology, we considered two key factors. First, the phenomenon of interest is learning from failures in software engineering, which is a contextual process influenced by an organization's unique practices, culture, past experiences, and commitment to continuous improvement (Leveson, 1995; Kalu et al., 2023) . Second, the limited prior work in this area meant there was insufficient foundation for designing a closed-ended survey. Answering our research questions would therefore require in-depth, descriptive responses best obtained through open-ended discussions, opinions, and detailed observations. Report issue for preceding element Given the preceding considerations, we therefore adopt a case study methodology for our research design and analysis. This approach enables us to investigate the phenomenon in depth in real-world context (Ralph et al., 2020) . For data collection, we took a semi-structured interview approach (Saldaña, 2021) , allowing us to compare results across subjects while providing the flexibility to capture emergent insights (DeJonckheere and Vaughn, 2019) . The resulting data is anticipated to be exploratory and grounded in the perspectives and experiences of practitioners. Report issue for preceding element 4.2. Case study setting: A space-related HRO Report issue for preceding element Our study is set at the Department of Flight Software at a major national space research center. This department researches and develops flight software, ranging from embedded software to payload software for aircraft and spacecraft. The department is also the contact point for software quality assurance for safety-critical space systems. The department currently supports around 15 space missions with its software expertise. Report issue for preceding element As a national space research center responsible for supporting mission-critical projects, it exemplifies characteristics of an HRO (Christianson et al., 2011) . This department operates in a high-risk context and shows sensitivity to operational failures. Its structure and mission mandate a commitment to resilience and deferring to technical expertise. The department's role in software development for space missions, along with its responsibility for software quality assurance, aligns with the expectations of an HRO that must prioritize reliability, continuous learning, and failure management. Given these properties, we hypothesized that the organization would exhibit practices for learning from software failures, making it an appropriate site to conduct our study. Protocol design and development Report issue for preceding element To develop the initial interview protocol, we studied the background and related literature ( section 2), which provided a solid foundation of existing knowledge on learning from failures and highlighted gaps that informed our research questions and subsequently our interview questions. We identified knowledge gaps and created the initial protocol, structured along typical postmortem and failure management process recommendations. Report issue for preceding element We refined the protocol through practice interviews (2 subjects) and pilot interviews (5 subjects). For practice, the lead author interviewed two graduate students with prior SE work experience to iteratively improve the interview questions. These practice interviews led to modifications across our interview protocol to improve depth and focus. In the pilot, we conducted 5 interviews with practitioners from various HRO industries. The subjects for the pilot study were recruited using convenience sampling, drawing from two groups: part-time professional master's students at our university (pilot interviews 1–3) and practitioners from the lead author's internship at another organization (pilot interviews 4–5). Report issue for preceding element During these pilot studies we gathered insight about how these practitioners and their organizations learn from failures. We include data from the pilot study where feasible to evaluate transferability of our findings from the case study at the center to other organizations. The pilot study interviews led to modifications to improve focus on failure recurrence, communication, and automation. Data analysis Report issue for preceding element Interview recordings were anonymized with classified information redacted, and were auto transcribed by www.rev.com. We conducted our analysis through a multi-stage coding process (Campbell et al., 2013) , followed by thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006) . Two analysts (A1 and A2) analyzed the transcripts. The lead author, Analyst A1, oversaw all stages of data collection, including protocol design and conducting interviews, and actively led every phase of the analysis. A1 and A2 are graduate students with research experience in software engineering and software failure analysis. The supervisors are scholars and practitioners in software engineering. (Campbell et al., 2013) and O'Connor & Joffe (O'Connor and Joffe, 2020) . Threats to validity Report issue for preceding element We outline limitations in our study by discussing construct, internal, and external threats to validity (Wohlin, 2012) as well as author positionality. Report issue for preceding element Construct Threats concern the alignment between our research methods and the concepts we intended to study. To mitigate such threats, we designed our interview protocol based on established literature on postmortems, failure analysis, and learning from failures in software engineering. Following established guidelines, we conducted both internal and external pilot interviews to ensure our our interview protocol would capture relevant data (Chenail, 2011) . Report issue for preceding element Internal Threats concern factors that may influence the validity of cause-and-effect interpretations. As with any qualitative research, data interpretation carries an inherent risk of subjectivity. To enhance the reliability of our findings, we employed an iterative approach to develop the codebook, involving a collaborative approach with multiple raters reviewing, discussing, and developing the codes, and achieved a high level of agreement. We recognize the potential for bias since the case study was conducted at an organization where one of the authors was employed as an intern. Report issue for preceding element External Threats limit generalizability to other contexts. We applied a case study methodology with semi-structured interviews. This method explores a phenomenon in depth at one site, gaining detailed insight at the cost of less generalizability. To provide some insight on the transferability of our findings, we supplemented our case study with interviews from subjects at four additional HROs. Report issue for preceding element Author Positionality refers to individual characteristics of the authors that may influence their analysis (Holmes, 2020) . The lead author worked as a summer research fellow and conducted interviews at the organization. Two other authors are employed at the center. A third author works at a comparable organization in another country. These professional positionalities afford strengths and weaknesses. Employment relationships and experience in similar working environments permit data collection and improved interpretation of contextual and technical aspects of the data. However, they also add biases to reflect an employer or a class of organization in a positive light. To mitigate that bias, all data analysis involved a second author-analyst with no relationship to these organizations. Report issue for preceding element
Results and Analysis Report issue for preceding element We organize our results into three themes that answer our research questions. Our analysis reveals both 6 new findings and 5 findings that have been partially discussed in prior works. The challenges faced by this organization to learn from failures mirror barriers in conducting postmortems identified in prior works: perceived low benefit, lack of time, funding, culture, procedure, and mechanisms for failure learning, and the tacit and situated nature of failure knowledge (Kasi et al., 2008) . We also observed additional challenges, including fragmented knowledge, difficulty recalling lessons, overconfidence in general practices, shifting responsibility to individuals (especially newcomers), concerns about bureaucratic processes, reliance on informal SDLCs, and knowledge loss from turnover. Theme 1: Failure learning is ad hoc Report issue for preceding element We report findings on how engineers gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures. Report issue for preceding element 5.1.1. Gathering lessons from failures is informal Report issue for preceding element Across all subjects, there was no structured or consistent process for gathering lessons learned from failures; instead, such efforts were ad hoc, informal, and dependent on personal initiative. All subjects stated that formal processes for reflection were absent. As S5 noted, “I don't think that we have a formal process on how to make sure that [failures] doesn't happen again.” Others echoed this absence, with S6 stating, “We don't have a very strict process…not really,” and S3 explaining that group reflection is not part of their routine: “There's not a big reflective period where we say, 'Okay, this error happened…how should we do it in the future?”' Even recalling a single such meeting proved difficult for some; S5 admitted, “I can't think of a single time where we just sat there and discuss…issue to avoid it in the future.” S1 emphasized “we study the problem in order to fix the problem and not…to retrieve some knowledge out of it.” Instead, participants described informal and improvised efforts to reflect on failures. These were typically team-driven and irregular, such as a one-time feedback meeting described by S8: “We're going to do this actually in a couple of weeks…but it's not standard.” S9 confirms, “We do it…on the fly…But negative point is of course that there's no real structure, it's more like sharing experiences.” Report issue for preceding element When it does occur, reflective practices are often initiated by individuals with personal motivation, rather than organizational norms. S4 explained, “I try to implement those kinds of good practices…documenting the issues, learning from them, to reflections…but not as part of the main software development process.” S5 emphasized that improvements often arise because someone personally pushes for them: “Steps…are not in a process defined by itself, but more on the personal motivation of some developers.” Participants also suggested that issues perceived as more critical, architectural, or linked to external reviews were more likely to trigger reflection. As S2 observed, “Issues are studied and lessons learned gathered based on criticality [determined by potential] damage.” Notably, such efforts often occurred at the discretion of senior members. As S2 noted, “Lessons learned…it's mainly up to…the seniors on the group.” While some senior members, like S7, took initiative to reflect on what went well or poorly, these efforts were neither widespread nor institutionalized: “Normally for me it's always necessary after a project to checking what's good, what was wrong, what would be better and so on…[but] this is not reviewed by this department.” Report issue for preceding element Notably, a few subjects described practices that revealed that they were confused about what constitutes a lesson learned, equating fixing bugs, documenting issues, or general discussions with reflective learning. As S6 explained, “There was this issue and the lesson learned will be…to fix this issue.” S10 also incorrectly interpreted postmortems, “we have a scrum meeting and we speak about the things…finished…the plans we have for the future…so that maybe counts as postmortem.” Report issue for preceding element 5.1.2. Documentation of lessons learned is inconsistent & fragmented Report issue for preceding element Most subjects (8/10) reported that documenting lessons learned was ad hoc, informal, and left to individual discretion. S4 explained, “when we close the issue, it is not mandatory to document what did you do…[it's a] personal decision. We don't have that process…I know from experience that this is important so I do it.” Half of the subjects indicated that lessons learned are not documented, as S6 admitted, “I don't think they are documented, pretty much at all.” Most subjects shared that they, “track issues, but not for lessons.” While some participants, like S3, acknowledged that documentation might happen occasionally, they emphasized the lack of a defined process: “Yes, it might be, but it's not like there's a process for that.” As S10 suggested, “Firstly maybe you have to standardize what you should write.” Report issue for preceding element In the absence of formal processes, subjects rely on tacit knowledge rather than accessible records. When asked about lessons learned, S10 stated, “Not really lessons learned, no. I think they're more like internally…we learned from it.” Likewise, S8 emphasized the implicit nature of such knowledge: “Not explicit, but implicit…you learn how to do it in a better way, but this is just internal knowledge.” S2, S6, and S10 shared examples where even when recurring issues are discussed, the knowledge is rarely written down, “I don't think we document it anywhere to be honest…Maybe it would be a good idea.” Report issue for preceding element Where documentation does occur, subjects described various methods used to capture failure knowledge, highlighting that it is distributed across multiple platforms. S5 explained that issue severity affects where documentation is placed: “If it's…bigger issues…then it might get its own GitLab issue…If it's just a local discussion…then I don't think it gets documented.” While GitLab was frequently mentioned as the primary repository for failure knowledge, many participants also referred to inconsistent practices of storing knowledge in supplementary tools. Other participants described failure documentation being scattered across “merge requests” ( S4), “meeting notes” ( S2), and “scientific documents…showing the lessons learned” ( S7). The most senior participant, S7, also reported contributing insights to a software engineering network wiki within the organization, “We have the software engineering network and several lessons learned inside…coming from…my experiences.” Although subjects mentioned wikis, they did not use or refer to them, showing how even widely available resources like wikis remain underutilized. S2 explained, “When you look to the wiki page called 'lesson learned from project,' it's a few, not every project has one.” Most participants were not well aware of these wikis, suggesting that such contributions may not be widely known or accessed. This fragmentation of knowledge is further discussed in section 5.3.4, where it poses barriers to application of knowledge and team coordination. Report issue for preceding element 5.1.3. Lessons learned are shared & applied informally Report issue for preceding element All case study participants reported that failure knowledge is shared through informal discussions rather than structured processes within teams. As S4 explained, “Not officially, but it is common that during the day I discuss something with my team members…but not in an official way.” S9 echoed “[It's] just on the go.” Most subjects mentioned stand-up meetings as a space to share blockers and informally learn from past issues: S3 stated, “[In] stand-up meetings… [we] discuss…obstacles…[and] if someone has had the problem before [they can share]…but it's not in every project.” Some subject also shared more casual discussion, as S2 noted, “Sometimes in coffee break…we had this issue and we tried to learn this…in short discussion…I learned from their lesson learned, then I personally will keep it in mind…when I face a similar problem.” While these informal discussions sometimes result in useful insights, they are not systematically shared. Report issue for preceding element Subjects shared that lessons often remains siloed within teams, as cross-project sharing is circumstantial. S1 admitted, “Very rarely we report in a bigger round…that we experienced a failure…So it completely stays in the team and will not be exchanged.” Others shared that external sharing depends on severity or criticality. As S2 explained, “If it's relevant for other projects or a very critical situation, then this information is spread.” While some reported knowledge carried over through individual mobility across projects, as noted by S3:“[If] I work in one project, get some problems, start working on another project, and of course I bring this experience and try to communicate this,” few described structured mechanisms to facilitate that transfer. Report issue for preceding element Subjects highlighted that the most consistent form of failure knowledge transfer occurred through informal mentorship from senior developers, who act as repositories of failure knowledge. All subjects cited S7 (the most experienced) as their go-to person for resolving issues. S9 emphasized this reliance: “I…have to ask [ S7 ]…[using] his experience and we can then decide how we want to avoid problems or…to improve.” S6 echoed, “[When] I have issue…I…just go to [ S7's] office and ask…how would you tackle it?…Normally it's knocking the door and asking, 'Hey, do you have experience on [this]?”' While this method was appreciated by junior developers, others noted its limitations. S1 highlighted that this one-person dependency led to inefficiencies resolving recurring failures: Report issue for preceding element “ A couple situations…where issues…were recurring…[and often] someone else on the team…[would recollect], 'Oh yeah, I remember I faced this too. You have to do…' So that's probably the worst case…to handle those things, but that's how it's done…You have to hope…[to find] someone [who remembers that they] already experienced it…there's not a process to look it up.” Report issue for preceding element The absence of a centralized source of knowledge meant that even recurring issues had to be rediscovered unless someone remembered them. As S1 put it, “This could happen only if the person who fixed it…is still there. I never experienced someone say, 'We had this issue before and fixed it like this.”' Report issue for preceding element Application of lessons learned was ad hoc, driven by individual initiative, and typically occurred only when subjects (5/10) recalled a similar issue or knew where to find relevant documentation. As S3 described, “I…couple of times…searched through the issues…to figure out…what I did last time…but it's not in the process…to prevent…these issues again.” S10 does the same, “[but] I don't know if other people do it.” S4 recounted using past issues for “training” and “to guide users to design their applications,” but acknowledge that it is a personal practice. Similarly, S9 says, “sometimes lesson learned…are referred when someone new joins…[but] it's not too often…last time…was two years ago.” S5 acknowledged that even with recurring issues, their issue notes are not referenced: “Issues appear over and over again…[however] issue…meeting notes are not really checked. I've…maybe twice or thrice.” Others expressed that referencing past documentation is not common practice: S8 remarked, “No, I think it's not so.” Report issue for preceding element To enable feedback cycles, S7 mentions questionnaires, though no other subject was aware of them, and they remain unused. To enable feedback cycles, S7 mentioned questionnaires: one to reflect on failures, “a questionnaire to discuss…what's wrong?…can this happen again?”, and another to apply lessons during, “design and getting…requirements…to get the criticality of system.” However, when we pointed out that no other subject referenced these, S7 admitted, “Normally you should use it always…But people don't…we cannot [enforce] everyone to do it,” and as a result, “failures are made again and again.” As S2 said, “You'll [have to] be self-motivated to search the wiki…GitLab, ask the seniors…otherwise…there's no process to bring lessons down to you.” Report issue for preceding element 5.1.4. Transferability Report issue for preceding element The pilot interviews matched the case study data: gathering, documenting, and applying lessons from failure were also ad hoc for those subjects. Report issue for preceding element Pilot participants confirmed that lessons from failures are gathered informally and often depend on personal motivation. PTeam described a one-off, “brainstorming workshop…where we discussed… production issues we have being observing…and not having those repeat again,” but this was an exception, not the norm. PS3 admitted, “I think I was the only one who would [reflect] such a thing.” Subjects explicitly stated the absence of formal reflection processes, with S5's acknowledgment that PS1 “I don't think we have a formal process to make sure that [failures] doesn't happen again.” PS1 echoed, “There's no process for that.” Report issue for preceding element Documentation practices in the pilot study mirrored the case study's inconsistencies, due to time and resource constraints. Theme 2: Recurring failures and the illusion of effective failure management Report issue for preceding element Although engineers often believed they manage failures effectively, recurring incidents reveal gaps that undermine this perception. Report issue for preceding element 5.2.1. Recurring failures persist due to lack of structured learning Report issue for preceding element All subjects reported facing the same types of issues multiple times, e.g., across different projects or development stages. S7, the most senior participant, noted, “failures are made again and again…the same problems.” S2 says that it happens across projects: “Same problems but different places because it's got a different project, different requirement.” S6 sees it across developmental phases, “we're…committing the same mistakes over and over just in different phases.” S9 complains that rather than preventing recurrence during development they fix it during maintenance: “We…face the same issues again instead of implementing it in a proper way.” Report issue for preceding element Most subjects recognized that recurring failures stem from a lack of structure in learning from failures. S1 described, “Couple situations…in the last couple of years where issues in our software…were recurring…again and again, but there wasn't a process to handle those…So that's probably the worst case, how to handle those, but that's how it was done actually.” Subjects allude recurrences occurred because lessons were not formally recorded, generalized, or communicated. S1 added, “after a while someone forgot…and this issue popped up…again…we discussed this already five months ago.” S2 recounts another example, “we found an issue…we fix it…and then we got this problem again…[because] fix [is not] discuss[ed] on the group level…others…were not aware about the issue at all.” S2 alludes to a deeper underlying issue, where problems are solved on a case-by-case basis rather without gathering generalized lessons: “The problem…[of] problems happening again and again because…we solve it by scenario based and every time we got to actually to solve it again…and we need to provide a generic solution, not scenario based solution.” Without structure practices to gather and communicate lessons, local fixes remain siloed, and failures reoccur. Report issue for preceding element 5.2.2. Confidence in general practices undermines the perceived need for learning from failures Report issue for preceding element Many participants believed that adherence to such general software engineering practices, was sufficient to prevent the recurrence of failures, reducing the perceived need for structured failure learning processes. S9 explained, “we have a structure there already…we have our requirements…a test-driven approach…guidelines, standards.” S3 described relying on, “coding guidelines…defensive programming…templates…unit tests…and peer review before merging…to avoid…errors…but it's not that I sift through [past] issues.” S6 emphasized frameworks were designed to prevent known issues: “we tried to come up with these reusable frameworks…because history repeats itself.” A few subjects, like S4, felt that documenting bug reports sufficed, “issue reappearing… [is] prevented by…adding example…bugs…for the issue.” S4 emphasized that general measures embed years of industry experience, and is sufficient to prevent recurring failures: Report issue for preceding element “ We use several general measures to avoid these…[recurring failures]…we rely on best practices, guides, standards…because those collect years of experience [and reflection] in software development from industry…That's what prevents…issues.” Report issue for preceding element However, some participants acknowledged limitations in relying solely on best practices. S7 criticized the superficiality of standards, “with the standards…the common agreement is…so weak…It didn't help you normally…[because] they don't share too much knowledge,” consistent with prior findings (Chen et al., 2024) . S1 noted another drawback, “[It's] a 150–200 page PDF with a lot of rules…even I can't tell you all the rules. [We] should know where this is, but not learned by heart…[it's] not [referenced] that often.” Consequently, the HRO standard they sometimes follow requires lessons learned to be gathered, but they do not seem to do it consistently, as S2 shares, “The standard from the…Space Agency…[requires us to] collect lesson learned…but this doesn't apply for all of our projects.” Report issue for preceding element Notably, some subjects attributed recurring failures to the inexperience of junior engineers — masking systemic gaps in how failure knowledge is transferred. S7 observed, “I see [failures] happened again and again because…young software engineers…didn't have the experience and the sharing of experiences is limited.” They defended their on-boarding process, claiming it mitigates these gaps: “There aren't really gaps, there's training. The only issue we have is time to get the lessons and [applying] the lessons. That's why we have an on‑boarding process.” Responsibility for avoiding recurring failures was shifted to individuals, particularly newcomers, rather than organizational processes to learn from failures. S7 explained, “as long as the process is understandable for new employees…for experienced engineers it might not be necessary…[they already have] a big knowledge base.” This assumption risks perpetuating knowledge silos and obscuring structural challenges in failure knowledge transfer. Report issue for preceding element 5.2.3. Ad hoc learning sometimes prevents recurrence—but is rare Report issue for preceding element In a few cases, serious or recurring failures prompted technical or process improvements, though these were typically driven by impactful incidents and individual initiative rather than structured processes. S7 described a recurring, “big fail[ure]…the project manager called and I hear…crying and…[he says,] 'everything is not working…we cannot start the satellite…nothing works'…[I respond], 'Yeah, I know this failure, this was fixed three months ago'…[Our] lessons learned…[from this failure led to] this modeling…group…and test process…[and] a research project.” In another case, S6 explained how a post-mission reflection led to improvements: “From results of mission one…we learn a lot… [and] we are applying it for the new mission.” In a different case, S2 recalled that after a recurring issue, the team collectively learned, documented a guideline, and shared it: “we…define a guideline…how to…and what not to do…to not get this problem,” and as a result the problem did not recur “because the people who joined afterwards, they follow the same instructions.” S3 noted that from a prior failure, they, “try to have a lot of checks beforehand…so later you don't have as many pitfalls.” Report issue for preceding element 5.2.4. Transferability Report issue for preceding element Pilot subjects agreed that recurring issues remain a problem due to the lack of structured processes for failure learning. PS4 admitted, “we come across the same issues over and over…it gets worse and worse”, echoing the case study's examples of unresolved issues resurfacing across projects. Pilot subjects described formal structures like root cause analysis and documented action items. Theme 3: Challenges within the learning-from-failures process Report issue for preceding element Themes 1 and 2 highlighted the ad hoc nature of learning from failures. Since many subjects were aware of the ill effects, we examine why more robust processes have not taken root. Report issue for preceding element 5.3.1. Lack of time and resources Report issue for preceding element All participants emphasized that limited time and competing responsibilities constrain their ability to engage in structured failure learning. Subjects describe wearing multiple hats at the center, as S2 listed, “we are not solely software developers, we're also researchers, sometimes project managers, systems engineers.” Each of them have various responsibilities as S1 outlined, “our job is not [just] the development, you have project meeting…paper stuff…usually more than one project…bureaucracy…some other additional tasks…deadlines.” S5 observed, “most employees are…drowned in project work.” As a result, time for such reflective processes is limited, as S1 questions, “I don't know if we would have time to designate to such a process.” Because, as S4 explained, “the main issue is time, because…you cannot…spend much time thinking about…issues.” All subjects echoed this, including S2: “sitting and reading or creating new incidents…will take a long time.” Report issue for preceding element S3 and S7 pointed out budget constraints: “You have only funding for project, and normally you [would be] doing lessons learned afterward.” As a result, S5 says, “I don't know if people would prioritize learn[ing].” S9 reflected on this trade-off, “it's a matter of how much time do you invest, creating a structure and how much use you have out of it.” S10 says that the team must believe in the trade-off, “I think the team has to see postmortems as sensible and worth the time.” Report issue for preceding element Concerns were also raised that formalized reflection meetings could become bureaucratic or burdensome. Per S4: Report issue for preceding element “ We do…project development…also research. Discussion and Future Work Report issue for preceding element This section reflects on whether current practices enable effective learning from failures, considers what broader software engineering practices could offer this HRO, highlights strengths of this HRO's practices, and outlines future directions. Report issue for preceding element 6.1. Learning from failure: Are we equipped? Report issue for preceding element This study set out to understand how software engineers learn from failures, with the assumption that HROs, such as a national space research center, would exemplify best practices (cf. section 4.2). We selected this case study precisely because we expected its teams to operationalize the structured, cyclic processes for capturing, disseminating, and applying failure knowledge that are prescribed in standards for high-reliability engineering (Christianson et al., 2011) . However, our findings challenge this assumption. Prior work proposed structured techniques for gathering, documenting, and sharing lessons from failures, with limited guidance on applying them (cf. section 2.2). In contrast, practices at this HRO were largely ad hoc: recommended techniques for gathering, documenting, and sharing were rarely followed, and offered little to learn about systematic application. While prior literature has recommended various structured practices for gathering, documenting, sharing, and applying lessons from failures (cf. section 2.2), practices at this HRO were ad-hoc. Despite operating in a high-stakes, reliability-critical environment, engineers at this HRO described largely informal, ad hoc, and inconsistent approaches to learning from software failures (cf. section 5.1). Many participants acknowledged recurring failures that were not systematically addressed, with lessons learned often confined to individual memory, undocumented conversations, or informal mentorship (cf. section 4.2). This pattern extended across our pilot study as well, which included subjects from other HROs and sectors (cf. section 5.1.4, section 5.2.4). Report issue for preceding element The challenges faced by this organization to learn from failures mirror barriers in conducting postmortems identified two decades ago (Kasi et al., 2008) (cf. section 5). The persistence of these long-standing obstacles, even in a mission-critical HRO, underscores how deeply entrenched such barriers are in software engineering practice. These findings raise a foundational question: If even such mission-critical HROs lack structured learning-from-failure practices, where should we look for models of effective failure learning? And a broader concern: even though failure analysis is widely recommended in both safety-critical systems and modern DevOps cultures, do software teams actually know when, why, and how to learn from failures effectively? Our results suggest that even in organizations theoretically aligned with the principles of continuous improvement and resilience, failure learning remains underdeveloped, reactive, and fragile. This disconnect points to a pressing need: the software engineering field may require clearer structures, practices, and cultural reinforcements to support failure learning. Without these, even well-intentioned teams may repeat the same mistakes, not due to negligence, but because they lack processes or tools for turning failures into actionable knowledge. Report issue for preceding element Future work could look to domains where incentives for avoiding recurring failures are more tightly coupled to financial, contractual, or regulatory obligations. For example, cloud infrastructure providers with stringent service level agreements (Beyer et al., 2016) , banking systems with financial repercussions and regulatory oversight (Pri, 2021) , or government agencies with centralized engineering governance (NAS, [n. d.]a; Les, 2024) . These settings may offer more developed models for learning from failures, due to their financial, contractual, or regulatory pressures. Report issue for preceding element 6.2. What could broader software engineering practices teach this HRO? Report issue for preceding element While our case site operates in a mission-critical domain, many of its failure reflection and documentation practices lag behind those seen in large IT organizations. Postmortem practices at some of these organizations, such as structured templates and searchable incident databases, are well documented in practice and literature (Beyer et al., 2016; Allspaw, 2012) . In contrast, subjects in our study described ad hoc gathering of lessons and fragmented documentation (cf. section 5.1). Report issue for preceding element Our results suggest that HROs could benefit from adapting practices from IT organizations, particularly in how they document and track incidents. Structured postmortem templates, searchable failure databases, and lightweight regular reflection could offer scalable processes to support failure learning without introducing excessive bureaucracy. Formalizing mentorship systems, on-boarding materials, or guidelines for recurring failure modes, could further strengthen resilience against knowledge loss. Report issue for preceding element Notably — as both the case study and pilot interviews revealed — even when lessons are documented, their application and integration into the SDLC was inconsistent. Similarly, even in large IT and DevOps organizations, where postmortems and tooling are sometimes adopted, it remains unclear whether these efforts lead to meaningful learning or sustained changes in their SDLC. This raises an important question: How do, and how can, learning-from-failure translate into sustained improvements in the SDLC? The feedback cycle from lessons to tangible process change remains poorly understood and, consequently, poorly supported by researchers. Report issue for preceding element 6.3. Learning from HROs: Strengths & opportunities Report issue for preceding element Despite gaps in structured failure learning, our results reveal informal practices that are worth preserving and enhancing. Given constraints, such as lack of time and resources (cf. section 5.3), subjects adapted to ad-hoc yet effective ways of gathering, sharing, and applying critical insights, particularly through informal discussions, senior-to-junior mentoring, and creating proven patterns (cf. section 5.1). Engineers, aware of the high-stakes nature of their work, developed habits of caution, defensive programming, and sought peer input, showing initiative even without formal process backing. The broader software engineering community, especially startups or agile teams where heavy process may be impractical, could learn from these flexible, human-centered approaches. However, as our results show, such informal systems are vulnerable to turnover, memory loss, and siloing (cf. section 5.3). Perhaps the challenge is not to replace them, but to understand: How can we augment them with lightweight structures that support consistency and institutional memory? Report issue for preceding element 6.4. Future directions Report issue for preceding element Based on the current practices (cf. section 5.1), their flaws (cf. section 5.2), and their challenges (cf. section 5.3) in learning from failures that we observed in our case study, we propose three opportunities for future research. Report issue for preceding element First, more research is needed to evaluate the impact of different failure learning practices, particularly the tradeoffs between lightweight, more ad hoc methods and structured approaches. When is informal reflection enough? When does structure pay off? What contextual factors ( e.g., team size, mission type) make one approach preferable over another? Report issue for preceding element Second, our data suggest the need to design and evaluate lightweight failure learning processes that support gathering, documentation, and reflection of lessons without imposing significant overhead. As participants repeatedly noted, time and resources are major constraints, even when the cost of failures ( i.e., the value of failure learning) is acknowledged. Milestone-based postmortems or team-driven reflection sessions may be more sustainable than rigid weekly reviews or top-down documentation mandates. Report issue for preceding element Third, automation tools may offer a promising avenue to reduce the burden of capturing and retrieving failure knowledge. There are existing tools to help manage incidents (Fir, [n. d.]; PagerDuty, 2023) , but they are limited help for leveraging lessons learned. Recent works have explored using LLMs to synthesize incident data, from various sources such as public reports (Anandayuvaraj et al., 2024) and logs (Ahmed et al., 2023) into postmortem reports, and to apply them to understand (Jin et al., 2023) , conduct root cause analysis (Zhang et al., 2024) , and suggest mitigation (Ahmed et al., 2023) for incidents. Combining such automated reports, with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020) techniques may also enable engineers to easily leverage failure data and lessons. For example, this could enable a failure-aware chatbot to provide engineers with targeted, context-sensitive advice grounded in past failures. However, as our findings highlight, existing tools such as wikis and questionnaires are often underutilized, suggesting that automation efforts must integrate seamlessly into developers' existing workflows to encourage adoption. Report issue for preceding element
Conclusion Report issue for preceding element Learning from failures is widely regarded as essential for improving reliability in high-stakes domains, however there is a knowledge gap on how it is done in practice. This study presents the first in-depth qualitative analysis of how software engineers in an HRO gather, document, share, and apply lessons from failures. Through interviews with 10 engineers at a space research center and 5 pilot interviews at other HROs, we found that failure learning is informal, ad-hoc, and inconsistently integrated into software development practices, even in settings where reliability is critical. Our findings reveal challenges including time and resource constraints, knowledge loss due to team turnover and fragmented documentation, and weak process enforcement. Although HROs and IT organizations are held as exemplars for structured postmortems, the feedback loop from documented insights to software process improvements remains poorly understood across domains. Report issue for preceding element Our findings shed light on the real-world practices, challenges, and organizational factors shaping failure learning practices within software teams in HROs, and suggests directions for designing better processes, tooling, and organizational structures to support failure-aware software development life-cycle.
