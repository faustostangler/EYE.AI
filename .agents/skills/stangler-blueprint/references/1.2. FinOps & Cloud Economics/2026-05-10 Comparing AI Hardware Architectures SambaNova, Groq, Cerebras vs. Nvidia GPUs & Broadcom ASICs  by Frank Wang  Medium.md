Overview: AI accelerators from SambaNova, Groq, and Cerebras introduce novel architectures (dataflow, tensor-streaming, wafer-scale) that challenge conventional Nvidia GPUs and custom Broadcom ASICs (e.g. Google TPUs). Below we compare them across key factors for large language model (LLM) training and inference.
Cost of Serving (TCO)
SambaNova: Provides a full-stack system (hardware + software + pre-trained models) aimed at reducing overall costs. By fitting large models on fewer nodes, SambaNova claims several-fold better total cost of ownership (TCO) than an equivalent GPU setup (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). For example, 16 SambaNova SN40L chips can replace 320 GPUs for serving a 671B parameter model (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar), drastically cutting the number of racks, power, and management overhead needed. This collapsed infrastructure reduces costs in data center space, cooling, and operational complexity (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar) (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar).
Groq: Groq’s inference-focused LPU (Language Processing Unit) is designed for efficiency at scale. Groq highlights competitive pricing, emphasizing lower cost per inference by delivering more performance at small batch sizes (where GPUs often under-utilize) () (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). In practice, a single Groq LPU card (≈$20k) can outperform expensive cloud GPU instances on LLM serving (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp). The company also offers a cloud service with simple integration (e.g. “ switch with 3 lines of code ”) to attract users, avoiding large upfront investments (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat).
Cerebras: Cerebras sells integrated systems (the CS series) built around its massive wafer-scale chips. Each unit is costly, but one Cerebras CS-3 can handle workloads that might require an entire GPU cluster, potentially saving on network infrastructure and licensing costs. Cerebras is positioning its solution at $0.10 per million tokens of inference (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat), aiming to undercut GPU cloud costs. Moreover, 20× faster inference at 1/3 the power means lower electricity bills for the same throughput (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat), further improving operational cost per output.
Nvidia GPUs: GPUs like the A100/H100 are expensive per unit and in high demand. Building capacity for large LLMs often means many GPUs (and licenses), driving up costs. However, their ubiquity and mature supply chain can lower some costs at scale. Cloud providers offer GPU instances on-demand, shifting CapEx to OpEx but at a premium per hour. Nvidia’s strong software stack (CUDA, libraries) also reduces development cost, an important facet of TCO beyond hardware price (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News).
Broadcom ASICs (Hyperscaler Chips): Custom AI ASICs designed with Broadcom (e.g. Google TPU, AWS Trainium) target optimal performance per dollar for specific workloads. Hyperscalers invest hundreds of millions upfront in design (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News), but at their scale this can yield lower marginal cost per inference/training in the long run. For instance, Google TPUv4 delivers 2× higher training performance per dollar vs. GPUs in some cases (Google TPUs vs. AWS Trainium & Inferentia vs. NVIDIA GPUs). These ASICs cut out the vendor margin and are tailor-made, so mature AI services (like translation or recommendation) can be served more cheaply once the chip is developed (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News). However, outside the big operators, others cannot easily leverage these cost benefits due to limited availability.
Throughput & Performance
Training and inference throughput (e.g. TFLOPs, tokens per second) and latency vary widely:
SambaNova (Reconfigurable Dataflow Architecture): SambaNova’s SN40L chip is a dataflow processor that streams data through compute units without constant context switching (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova) (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). This yields high hardware utilization. In a recent benchmark, 16 SN40L chips achieved 198 tokens/s per user on a 671B-param LLM (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar) — a model that would ordinarily need 40 racks of GPUs. SambaNova reports 3× the speed and 5× the efficiency of leading GPUs on this large inference task (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar) (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar). For training, SambaNova’s architecture is also designed to sustain throughput by eliminating the GPU’s kernel launch overhead, keeping the execution pipeline full (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova) (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). The trade-off is that raw TFLOP numbers aren’t directly comparable (a “ 1 PFLOP ” dataflow chip might outperform a “ 5 PFLOP ” GPU if the GPU is memory-bound).
(Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova) SambaNova’s dataflow architecture maps an entire model graph onto the chip, so intermediate results stream directly between compute units instead of shuttling to external memory after each layer. This massively reduces idle time and boosts throughput (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova) (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova).
Groq (Tensor Streaming Processor): Groq’s TSP architecture focuses on low-latency, high-throughput inference, especially at small batch sizes. The Groq LPU is a single massive core with 750 TOPS INT8 and 188 TFLOPs FP16 peak (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp), paired with 230 MB on-chip SRAM for model data (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp). Unlike GPUs, performance doesn’t drop at batch size 1 — in fact, Groq reported being 17.6× faster than an Nvidia V100 at batch=1 (and ~2.5× at large batch) in image inference (). For LLMs, a single Groq card hits 300 tokens/sec on Llama2–70B and ~750 tokens/sec on smaller 7B models (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp). This outpaces equivalent single-GPU setups (an H100 yields roughly tens of tokens/sec on 70B at low batch). Groq achieves top throughput and near-best latency on the independent LLMPerf leaderboard for Llama-2 models (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp). However, Groq currently excels at inference; it’s less proven for training throughput on huge models (due to lack of deep learning training optimizations like error-correcting codes, etc., which GPUs have).
Cerebras (Wafer-Scale Engine): Cerebras WSE-3 is a 125 PFLOP AI processor with 900,000 cores on one wafer (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). It can keep an extraordinary amount of the model’s computations on-chip, enabling extremely high token throughput when models fit in its on-chip memory. Cerebras has demonstrated 1,800 tokens/sec on an 8B Llama model (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat), and partners report 20× faster inference than GPUs on large models, transforming latency-sensitive applications (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Even the distilled 70B DeepSeek model ran 57× faster on Cerebras than on GPUs in one case (Cerebras Now The Fastest LLM Inference Processor; Its Not Even …). For training, Cerebras can often train moderate-sized models faster than multi-GPU clusters because it avoids the communication overhead of data parallelism — the entire model can be trained on one chip synchronously. For very large models exceeding on-chip memory, Cerebras uses a technique called weight streaming (reading weights from external MemoryX boxes), which sacrifices some speed but still allows single-wafer execution. In summary, on per-chip throughput, Cerebras currently leads for giant models, but scaling beyond one chip involves linking multiple wafers (which Cerebras supports up to 2048 wafers in a cluster) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat).
Nvidia GPUs: Nvidia’s Hopper H100 GPU is a powerhouse with ~1,000 TFLOPs of INT8 (with sparsity) and specialized Transformer Engine support (H100 has 4.6x A100 Performance in TensorRT-LLM, achieving …). In practice, an H100 can generate on the order of 50–100 tokens/sec on GPT-3 class models (depending on precision and batching) and can train models with tens of billions of parameters efficiently using data parallel and tensor parallel schemes. GPUs shine in mixed workloads and scale-out scenarios: connecting many GPUs via NVLink/InfiniBand can increase throughput nearly linearly for training (with software frameworks to manage parallelism). However, for pure inference throughput, GPUs are now being matched or exceeded by the above specialized designs. Even Nvidia admits H100 can’t match the single-chip speed of Cerebras or Groq on LLM inference (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Still, GPUs remain extremely versatile performers, and with large batch sizes or model ensembles, they can reach high throughput (albeit with higher latency per query in those scenarios).
Broadcom ASICs (e.g. TPUs): Google’s TPU v4 (built with Broadcom’s help) delivers 275 TFLOPs BF16 per chip and 1.2 TB/s memory bandwidth (Google TPUs vs. AWS Trainium & Inferentia vs. NVIDIA GPUs), and these chips are deployed by the thousands. A TPU v4 Pod of 4096 chips reaches 1.1 ExaFLOP aggregate compute (Google TPUs vs. AWS Trainium & Inferentia vs. NVIDIA GPUs). In MLPerf training benchmarks, TPUv4 and Nvidia A100 were in the same class of throughput, and upcoming TPU v5 is expected to be even faster. For inference, TPU-based systems can serve models like PaLM efficiently at Google scale, but their strength is often in training throughput for well-structured models (transformers, CNNs). Other ASICs like AWS’s Inferentia2 aim for high inference throughput per dollar rather than absolute performance. Overall, ASICs excel when fully utilized on their target workload — e.g. a recommendation model ASIC might do just that extremely well. But for varied LLM tasks, their throughput is impressive mostly when the model aligns with the chip’s design (matrix operations, etc.). If not, performance can degrade as certain operations run sub-optimally.
Memory & Model Loading
Memory capacity and bandwidth determine how large a model can be served or trained without partitioning, and how fast data can move:
SambaNova: The SN40L chip uses a three-tier memory architecture purpose-built for gigantic models (SambaNova SN40L RDU for Trillion Parameter AI Models — ServeTheHome) (SambaNova SN40L RDU for Trillion Parameter AI Models — ServeTheHome). Each chip has 520 MB of on-chip SRAM, 64 GB of High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) (~2 TB/s bandwidth), and can address up to 1.5 TB of off-chip DDR as a third tier (SambaNova SN40L RDU for Trillion Parameter AI Models — ServeTheHome). In a multi-chip SambaNova node (e.g. 16 chips), this scales to ~8 GB SRAM and 1 TB HBM pooled, meaning models with trillions of parameters can be accommodated in one node by spilling to DDR (SambaNova SN40L RDU for Trillion Parameter AI Models — ServeTheHome). SambaNova’s software automatically manages the tiers, keeping active working sets on-chip to avoid slowdown. The key benefit: a full model can be loaded onto a single accelerated system, avoiding the complex sharding needed for GPUs (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova) (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). By contrast, a large GPU deployment must partition a model across many 80 GB GPU memories, incurring coordination overhead. SambaNova essentially trades some latency (if DDR is hit) for capacity, but it claims even 1T-parameter models run smoothly on one SN40L-powered server (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova).
Groq: The Groq LPU lacks large external memory like HBM; instead it packs a big local SRAM (230 MB) on-die (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp) and relies on compiling models to fit that memory. This is sufficient for models up to ~Billions of parameters with compression/quantization, but something like a 70B model (at FP16 ~140 GB) clearly won’t fit in one chip. Groq addresses this by partitioning across multiple chips with deterministic data streaming between them. Their hardware has extremely fast chip-to-chip links (no caches or OS in the way) and a global compile that schedules communication, so multiple Groq cards can act in concert for larger models. Still, memory is a limiting factor: one Groq chip can serve a 70B model by itself only by using 8-bit weights or other tricks (as evidenced by Groq running Llama2–70B at 300 tok/s, likely with int8 weights) (Groq LPU AI Inference Chip is Rivaling Major Players like NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel | TechPowerUp). In general, Groq favors speed over capacity — it’s ideal when models or portions of models can reside in ~hundreds of MB on-chip memory (plus stream in activations). For giant models, GPUs or Cerebras (with more memory or ability to swap from off-chip) might handle them more straightforwardly.
Cerebras: The WSE-3 wafer has a massive 44 GB of SRAM on-chip (Product — Chip — Cerebras) (Product — Chip — Cerebras), giving 880× more fast memory than an H100 GPU (Product — Chip — Cerebras). This means models up to ~40 billion parameters (with 16-bit weights) can fit entirely on-chip, enabling them to be run without ever touching off-chip memory. This is a huge advantage: each core gets single-cycle access to weights and activations (Product — Chip — Cerebras), resulting in enormous memory bandwidth (21 PB/s aggregate on-chip) (Product — Chip — Cerebras). When models exceed 44 GB, Cerebras uses a technique called Weight Streaming — the model parameters reside in external MemoryX cabinets (which can be many TB), and the wafer streams in the needed weights each layer. This allows Cerebras to support models up to <24 trillion parameters> (as claimed for CS-3 clusters) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). The catch is that when streaming from off-chip, performance depends on that external memory bandwidth and the sparsity/pattern of weight access. But importantly, Cerebras eliminates the need for model parallel sharding in many cases — even a 175B parameter model (e.g. GPT-3) was run on a single CS-2 using MemoryX (with a latency hit, but it ran). This greatly simplifies scaling large models, at the cost of requiring Cerebras’ custom solution for memory.
(Product — Chip — Cerebras) A Cerebras Wafer-Scale Engine (WSE) chip is 56× larger than a high-end GPU, packing orders of magnitude more on-chip memory and cores (Product — Chip — Cerebras). This wafer-scale integration lets entire large models reside in fast on-chip SRAM, avoiding the memory bottlenecks of traditional architectures.
Nvidia GPUs: Modern GPUs rely on high-bandwidth VRAM (HBM). Nvidia’s A100 has up to 80 GB HBM, and H100 also 80 GB (with higher bandwidth). This is far less than the size of today’s largest models, so models must be split across multiple GPUs. Techniques like pipeline and tensor parallelism partition the model’s layers and weight matrices across GPUs. For example, a 175B GPT-3 might be split across 8×80GB GPUs (with each GPU holding ~22B params plus optimizer states). This adds complexity: GPUs need to frequently communicate to exchange activations or gradient updates, which slows training as models scale. For inference, loading a multi-hundred-GB model can incur significant overhead and requires orchestration (e.g., FastRPC or Triton inference server model partitioning). Simply put, a single GPU cannot hold the largest LLMs, so scalability comes from multi-GPU clusters with high-speed interconnects and careful software. Nvidia has mitigated some of this with features like NVLink (direct GPU-GPU memory copy) and MIG (splitting GPUs for smaller models), but memory remains a constraint — 80GB is only a “small fraction” of what modern generative models require (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova).
Broadcom ASICs (TPUs, etc.): TPU v4 has 32 GB HBM per chip (Google TPUs vs. AWS Trainium & Inferentia vs. NVIDIA GPUs). Google’s approach to large models is to leverage their ultra-fast interconnect (a custom 3D torus mesh) to spread models across thousands of TPUs (Google TPUs vs. AWS Trainium & Inferentia vs. NVIDIA GPUs). In effect, Google solved memory limits with scale-out: a TPU v4 pod can handle models with trillions of parameters by dividing the weights among chips and using the torus network (which provides tens of TB/s bisection bandwidth) to all-reduce and coordinate. This works but only within Google’s tightly controlled datacenters. Outside Google, AWS’s Trainium has 16×32GB = 512GB total per instance (8 chips per device), which can host moderately large models in memory, but still might need model parallelism for the largest ones. In summary, custom ASICs often face the same memory vs. model-size challenge as GPUs — they rely on either bigger clusters or lower precision. The difference is some are designed with certain model sizes in mind (e.g., an ASIC for recommendation might include just enough memory for the embedding tables it needs).
Software Ecosystem & Usability
SambaNova: A key selling point for SambaNova is its full-stack approach. They deliver the SambaNova Suite with pre-integrated models and a software environment so that users do not need to write low-level code (no CUDA required) to use the hardware (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). The SambaFlow compiler and runtime take models defined in standard machine learning frameworks (they support PyTorch, TensorFlow interfaces) and compile them into dataflow graphs for the RDU. For the user, deploying an LLM on SambaNova can be as simple as using their pre-trained model API or training with their optimized libraries. This makes it relatively easy to get started — the system comes ready to run in minutes on-premises (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). However, because SambaNova offers a vertically integrated solution, there is less community-driven tooling available. Users may rely on SambaNova’s support for any new model architectures or custom layers. The positive side is SambaNova hides much complexity; the downside is potential lock-in to SambaNova’s ecosystem (their models, their compilers). Still, for enterprises that just want to “use GPT-like models” without tweaking kernels, this black-box ease can be appealing.
Groq: Groq provides an SDK and compiler (GroqFlow) that takes neural network models (from TensorFlow, PyTorch via ONNX export, etc.) and compiles them for the Groq chip. No hand-written kernels are needed, but model code might require some adjustments to be supported by their compiler. They emphasize user-friendliness: for instance, to use the Groq Cloud, you essentially swap a few lines in your code (pointing model execution to Groq’s runtime) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Groq’s software stack is more limited in maturity compared to Nvidia’s — certain complex or dynamic operations might not be supported yet, requiring workarounds. The deterministic nature of the hardware (no caches, no randomness) simplifies performance tuning but demands that the compiler schedule everything exactly. As a result, developers may need to use Groq’s recommended patterns to get the best performance. Overall, the Groq ecosystem is growing (they’ve made headlines by topping inference leaderboards, attracting attention), but it is still a proprietary platform. Companies considering Groq should expect some engineering effort to integrate it into their pipeline, though initial trials are made easy via cloud access. In short: usable for inference (with available model examples and libraries), but for training or highly custom research models, one might hit software limitations that require waiting for Groq’s updates.
Cerebras: Cerebras initially required using TensorFlow and their frameworks to train models on the Wafer-Scale Engine. Over time, they have built out their software stack to be more user-friendly. They offer Cerebras Software 2.0 which includes integration with popular tools (e.g., PyTorch via framework bridges, support for Hugging Face models, etc.), and even a Model Zoo of large models they have trained (like Cerebras-GPT). They also introduced an Inference SDK for easier deployment of models on their hardware (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Nevertheless, compared to GPUs, the Cerebras software ecosystem is young. Users may need to adjust their training scripts to fit memory constraints (for on-chip execution) or to handle streaming mode. Debugging on a wafer-scale machine can also be non-trivial — there’s less community knowledge out there. On the plus side, Cerebras works with major AI players to integrate common tools (they mention partnerships integrating with LangChain, Weights & Biases, Docker containers for models, etc. to fit into MLOps pipelines) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Deploying a Cerebras for inference can be as simple as sending REST API calls to their server (they have a cloud as well). But for on-prem use, expect a learning curve for the IT team. Summarily, Cerebras’ ecosystem is improving, but Nvidia’s CUDA and libraries remain far more comprehensive for most tasks.
Nvidia GPUs: Unmatched software support is a major reason for Nvidia’s dominance (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News). CUDA, cuDNN, TensorRT, and numerous optimized libraries mean that almost every AI framework (TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX, MXNet, etc.) runs out-of-the-box on Nvidia GPUs. Developers benefit from a vast amount of documentation, forums, and existing code. High-level tools like PyTorch Lightning, Hugging Face Transformers, etc., all assume GPU usage by default. This makes GPUs extremely easy to adopt — from research to production, the path is well-trodden. The rich ecosystem also includes monitoring/profiling tools, multi-GPU orchestration (NCCL for communication), and continuous integration with new model architectures. Flexibility in software is a hallmark: you can run legacy code, mixed HPC + ML workloads, etc., on the same GPU. The downside for GPUs is mainly the need for CUDA knowledge for custom kernels (which most users avoid by using libraries) and the complexity of optimizing large distributed runs (which is more of a systems challenge than a software availability issue). In contrast, any new chip (like SambaNova, Groq, Cerebras) will initially lack this breadth of software maturity — Nvidia simply has a decade+ lead.
Broadcom ASICs (TPUs & others): Software ecosystem varies by chip and who it’s built for. Google TPUs are programmed via TensorFlow and JAX using the XLA compiler. That’s a solid ecosystem if you use those frameworks (Google has optimized many models on TPU), but notably PyTorch support on TPU is limited/experimental. This means a team that has built everything in PyTorch might have to rewrite or at least migrate to JAX/TF to fully leverage TPU, which is an adoption barrier (OpenAI building first custom AI inference chip with TSMC and …). TPU kernels also need to be XLA-compatible; some niche operations might not compile. AWS Trainium/Inferentia come with the Neuron SDK which integrates with PyTorch and TensorFlow (via custom runtime). AWS’s goal is to make using Trainium nearly transparent — but still, some ops may not be supported and require using a different implementation. Generally, custom ASICs require using custom compilers (XLA, Neuron, etc.), which is an extra layer of complexity compared to the well-known CUDA stack. Moreover, these ASICs are typically available only on specific clouds. So, while hyperscalers tailor software for their own ASICs (Google has a rich internal ecosystem for TPUs, Amazon for Trainium), for external users the ecosystem can feel fragmented or less flexible. In sum, Nvidia’s ecosystem remains the gold standard, with others racing to offer easier usability for their chips (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News) (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News).
Flexibility (Model Adaptability)
SambaNova: The Reconfigurable Dataflow Architecture is quite general within AI — it can be reprogrammed for vision CNNs, NLP transformers, recommendation MLPs, etc. There is no fixed function: the “reconfigurable” pipelines can implement arbitrary compute graphs (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). This gives SambaNova flexibility to support new model types via software updates (as long as the model can be expressed as a dataflow of known operations). Notably, SambaNova’s hardware was purpose-built for AI and especially large generative models (Sambanova vs Nvidia: AI Chipsets Compared | SambaNova). It may not run non-ML workloads well (no one is using RDUs for databases or cryptography, for example), but within AI, it’s adaptable. One limit might be if a model requires operations or sparsity patterns that the SambaNova compiler doesn’t handle efficiently — but the company can optimize those over time. Compared to a GPU, which can run any code (even non-AI code) though not always optimally, SambaNova is less flexible in that broader sense. However, compared to a fixed ASIC, SambaNova is much more flexible: you don’t need a new chip to support a new model or network architecture, you just compile it differently. Thus it strikes a balance between general-purpose and specialization, leaning toward the latter for performance reasons.
Groq: Groq’s single-core TSP design is programmable via software — it isn’t hard-wired to any specific neural network. This means Groq chips can support a wide variety of models, from transformers to CNNs to reinforcement learning policies, as long as you can compile them. The architecture is especially well-suited for dense, feed-forward computations (matrix multiplies, vector ops) done in a known sequence. It’s less suited for highly dynamic models with unpredictable control flow because everything is scheduled in advance. For LLMs and vision models (which mostly consist of predictable layers), Groq is very flexible. If tomorrow a new model architecture emerges, Groq would just need to update the compiler to support any new primitives; the hardware can handle it since it has lots of ALUs and uses software to orchestrate. That said, Groq is purely focused on inference right now. Training requires more flexibility in terms of data movement (gradients, optimizer state) and fault tolerance (since long training jobs can’t be one static schedule in practice). It’s not clear how easily the current Groq design can adapt to efficient large-scale training (though in theory it could run the math). In terms of future-proofing, Groq’s philosophy is that a simple deterministic core will remain useful for many model types. So far, it’s proving true in inference benchmarks.
Cerebras: Cerebras WSE is essentially a giant array of compute cores with a flexible fabric — it’s quite agnostic to model type. Users have run convolutional nets, transformers, sparse models, even non-ML workloads (like fluid dynamics simulations) on it (Cerebras Unveils Wafer Scale Engine Two (WSE2) — AnandTech). The size of the chip actually gives it more flexibility in one sense: it can accommodate whole models that other devices cannot, and can implement large parallel operations easily. Cerebras does particularly well with large dense models. If a model has a lot of irregular parallelism or needs fine-grained dynamic execution, Cerebras might not use resources as efficiently (since the cores are identical and optimized for typical neural net ops). But overall, any model that can be described in TensorFlow/PyTorch can in theory be mapped to Cerebras. They even support Mixture-of-Experts and sparse models by leveraging the vast on-chip memory to store routing tables, etc. The main flexibility limitation is scaling down: running very small models on Cerebras is possible but not cost-effective — the hardware is overkill. In contrast, a GPU can flexibly be sliced (via Multi-Instance GPU) to run many small jobs at once. Cerebras is really optimized for big models and big batches. In summary, within the domain of AI, Cerebras hardware is broadly flexible and can be repurposed for new model types as needed (no need for a new chip for each model), but it’s not a general-purpose processor for arbitrary workloads.
Nvidia GPUs: The most flexible in terms of supported workloads. GPUs are programmable devices that handle graphics, HPC, AI, and more. For AI specifically, Nvidia’s support for diverse data types (FP64 down to INT4, plus tensor cores for matrices) means GPUs can accelerate almost any kind of neural network to some degree. Whether it’s CNNs, RNNs, transformers, graph neural nets, or something exotic, developers have found ways to implement it on GPUs. If a new layer type doesn’t have a library support, one can write a CUDA kernel for it. This flexibility extends to non-neural algorithms too — many ML algorithms (like tree-based models) run on GPUs. The cost of this flexibility is extra power consumption (the GPU has a lot of circuitry that might not be used by a given AI workload) and complexity (developers have to manage threads, memory, etc., for custom code). But for end users running standard frameworks, the GPU feels universally compatible. It’s also forward-compatible: new network designs almost always target GPUs first (as researchers all have GPUs), so Nvidia’s hardware is rarely left behind by a new AI trend. In contrast, an ASIC designed around, say, dense matrix multiplies might struggle to efficiently execute a model that uses heavy recursion or sparse operations. Thus, GPUs are the safe bet for “can it run this model?” — the answer is nearly always yes (maybe not at optimal speed, but it will run).
Broadcom ASICs: By definition, application-specific ICs trade off some flexibility for efficiency. Google’s TPUs, for example, are highly optimized for matrix multiplies and transformer-like operations. They are less flexible in supporting arbitrary model control flow — certain RNNs or dynamic conditions needed special handling. Each TPU generation adds more flexibility (TPU v4 added support for embeddings and sparse features better than v2/v3 had). But compared to GPUs, TPUs are more constrained — if your model strays from the well-trod path of supported ops, you may not be able to run it efficiently. Similarly, AWS Inferentia is built for common inference patterns; if you try something unusual, it might not map well. Broadcom’s strategy has been to tailor chips to each hyperscaler’s needs (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News) (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News). This means each such ASIC is flexible for that company’s AI workloads (which are broad but not infinite). Outside hyperscalers, other companies likely won’t design their own ASIC due to cost, so they’ll use GPUs or one of these existing ASICs if available. In general, ASICs excel at mature, well-understood models (e.g. BERT, vision models, GPT-style transformers) where the patterns are fixed. They are less adaptable if the AI research community pivots to a radically different kind of model that wasn’t envisioned during design. GPUs and reconfigurable architectures would catch on quicker in that scenario.
Power Efficiency
SambaNova: By reducing data movement (one of the biggest contributors to AI energy use), SambaNova’s dataflow chips achieve higher performance per watt on large models. In the DeepSeek example, SambaNova reported 5× better energy efficiency than GPU alternatives (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar). Instead of hundreds of GPUs at ~300W each, they used 16 chips (each around a few hundred Watts) to do the same job faster (SambaNova hits 198 tokens per second on the full, non-distilled DeepSeek-R1 671B with only 16 SN40L RDU chips | TechRadar). Also, SambaNova’s ability to do more in a single chip or node reduces the need for power-hungry networking gear between many GPUs. It’s worth noting the SN40L is built on 5nm, a cutting-edge process, which helps power efficiency as well (SambaNova SN40L RDU for Trillion Parameter AI Models — ServeTheHome). That said, absolute power draw of a fully loaded SambaNova rack is still significant (these are high-performance processors). But if one SN40L node replaces an entire GPU cluster, the net watt-hours per inference goes down. Summary: SambaNova offers strong efficiency gains for large workloads by eliminating wasteful memory transfers and using fewer total chips — translating to potentially lower electricity and cooling costs for the same work.
Groq: Groq’s design was heavily influenced by power-efficiency considerations at low batch. By avoiding the overhead of control logic and caches, Groq uses its silicon for pure compute and deterministic data flow, which can be more energy-efficient. Groq claims up to 10× more efficient compute (performance per watt) compared to GPUs on certain inference tasks (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). The Groq chip’s thermal design power (TDP) is around 150W, and it delivers 750 INT8 TOPS, which is ~5 TOPS/W — quite competitive. In real LLM workloads, Groq’s advantage is that it doesn’t need to “over-provision” for batch size; a GPU might be underutilized (wasting power) if queries come sequentially, whereas Groq can sustain high utilization even at one query at a time. This leads to lower energy per inference at low batch. On large batches, GPUs approach their peak throughput (and thus efficiency), narrowing the gap. It’s also notable that Groq’s static scheduling can allow the chip to power down unused units intelligently if a model doesn’t need them at a moment, potentially saving power. Overall, Groq has positioned itself as green AI hardware — in one independent test, it had among the best power efficiency for LLM inference, second only to Cerebras in tokens/Joule.
Cerebras: Cerebras is surprisingly efficient for its size. By keeping everything on one chip, it avoids the energy overhead of moving data between GPUs (which involves SERDES transceivers, switches, etc., that burn a lot of power) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat) (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Cerebras reports that for the same model, their solution used 1/3 the power of an Nvidia DGX system while delivering much higher speed (Cerebras Redefines Speed with Fastest Inference for Next Agentic AI). The wafer-scale engine runs at relatively modest clock frequencies to manage thermals, which also helps efficiency (it’s spreading work across many cores rather than pushing a few cores to extreme clocks). Additionally, the high utilization of on-chip memory vs. off-chip means energy isn’t wasted waiting on memory transactions — nearly every cycle does useful work. One must consider the full picture: a Cerebras CS-2 consumes around 15 kW of power, which is huge for a single “chip,” but if that replaces, say, 64 GPUs that would have consumed 30+ kW together, it’s a win. Also, Cerebras can often use lower precision without loss (due to custom numeric formats), further boosting effective throughput/Watt. In summary, Cerebras offers excellent energy efficiency for large models: doing more with the watts it draws, even though the absolute power is large, the work done per joule is higher than traditional solutions.
Nvidia GPUs: Nvidia has improved GPU efficiency steadily (H100 is more efficient than A100, which is more efficient than V100, etc.). Features like the Transformer Engine (which dynamically uses FP8) can give up to 4.5× inference speed-up at similar power (H100 has 4.6x A100 Performance in TensorRT-LLM, achieving …), effectively improving efficiency. However, in absolute terms, GPUs remain power-hungry. A high-end H100 SXM can draw ~700W at load. In a server with 8 of them, that’s over 5.6 kW for one node. If you need dozens of such nodes, power and cooling become a major concern. GPUs have a lot of transistors dedicated to graphics and generality that aren’t needed for pure ML, which is “wasted” power for LLM use. Nonetheless, their flexibility and reuse across workloads often justify the power cost for users. In data centers, GPU nodes often have advanced cooling (liquid or evaporative) to handle these loads. In terms of perf/Watt, specialized chips now lead, but GPUs are not far behind and typically catch up within a generation or two for a given precision. And since GPUs can handle lower precision (INT8, FP8), one can trade accuracy for much higher efficiency if the application allows. The bottom line is that if maximizing throughput per watt is the priority and one is willing to use new hardware, alternatives like Groq and Cerebras can currently beat GPUs by a substantial margin (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). Nvidia is likely to narrow this gap with future architectures (and by leveraging its advantage in 5nm/4nm process and chiplet designs like the upcoming MI300-class GPUs).
Broadcom ASICs: ASICs generally achieve top-tier efficiency by cutting out all unnecessary logic — they are streamlined for matrix math, which gives them a high TOPS/W rating. Google TPU v4, for instance, is known to be extremely power-efficient for transformer workloads; Google has published that TPU v4 is 1.7× more performance per watt than V100 GPU in some benchmarks (and likely similar vs A100). The TPU v5e is advertised at 2.5× better inference performance per dollar (Google TPUs vs. AWS Trainium & Inferentia vs. NVIDIA GPUs), which implies improved efficiency as well (since cost often correlates with power usage in data centers). AWS’s Inferentia claims large perf-per-watt gains over using CPUs or even GPUs for inference, thanks to fixed-function optimizations for common operations. However, not all ASICs are automatically efficient — if an ASIC is built on an older process or ends up underutilized (e.g., a chip has 100 TOPS but your model can only use 50 TOPS of it due to bottlenecks), then you’re powering transistors that aren’t doing work. In an ideal scenario with a well-matched workload, ASICs deliver the best efficiency — that’s why hyperscalers pursue them: to cut their enormous electricity bills for AI services. Yet, outside those environments, one might not always fully utilize an ASIC’s capabilities, in which case the real-world efficiency could be lower. By contrast, GPUs can be re-purposed to different tasks to keep them busy. So, efficiency can be context-dependent. But purely on technical merits, a custom ASIC designed with AI in mind (and manufactured at scale) will usually have a performance-per-watt edge over a general-purpose GPU (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News) (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News).
Integration Challenges
SambaNova: Adopting SambaNova involves bringing in a new piece of infrastructure that is different from the standard GPU server. SambaNova delivers their hardware as an appliance or through their cloud. On-premise integration means installing SambaNova racks, which use standard power and cooling (19-inch racks, air-cooled) but you’ll need to ensure your data center can handle the power density (Why SambaNova’s SN40L Chip is The Best for Inference). There’s also the aspect of connecting the SambaNova system to your existing data pipeline — e.g., feeding data to it for training or hooking it up to your application for inference. SambaNova’s full-stack nature helps here: they aim to make the system “just work” once installed, and provide support for connecting via REST APIs or SDKs. One challenge could be retraining staff — engineers used to writing PyTorch on GPUs might need to learn SambaNova’s way of doing things or at least trust SambaNova’s automated optimizations. Another integration consideration is vendor lock-in: once your models and pipelines are tuned for SambaNova, switching back to another platform could require rework. However, from an ML pipeline perspective, SambaNova tries to be seamless: for example, fine-tuning one of their pre-trained LLMs on your data might only involve writing a small Python script, while SambaNova’s system handles the parallelism and performance details. In summary, integrating SambaNova is a “big decision” (new vendor, new hardware), but they mitigate the friction by delivering a turnkey solution with familiar interfaces.
Groq: Integrating Groq can be relatively easy for inference workloads that are already in a framework like TensorFlow or PyTorch — Groq’s compiler can ingest models from these frameworks. If using the Groq Cloud, integration is just via API calls or slight code changes, which is very low-friction (How Cerebras is breaking the GPU bottleneck on AI inference | VentureBeat). For on-premise use, Groq provides PCIe accelerator cards (often via partners like Dell or Supermicro servers). Slotting a Groq card into a server is similar to adding a GPU or FPGA card. Challenges include ensuring your model is supported by Groq’s toolchain (you might have to wait if you use a very new model architecture). Also, Groq is a smaller company, so their support and documentation, while improving, are not as extensive as Nvidia’s. Integrating into an existing ML pipeline might require writing a conversion script to ONNX and then compiling, which adds a step to your CI/CD. There’s also the question of scale: if you need to serve many queries, you might need multiple Groq cards — figuring out the scheduling of models across cards, load balancing, etc., might need custom handling (whereas Nvidia has well-known solutions for multi-GPU serving). Risk factor: as a relatively new entrant, some organizations might be hesitant to rely on Groq long-term until it’s more established. But technically, adding Groq to a pipeline is doable and has been proven by their wins on LLM inference leaderboards (which required integrating with open-source model code). One must plan for testing and validation though — outputs might have minor differences due to different numeric precision, so QA is needed when swapping hardware.
Cerebras: Integrating a Cerebras system is a bigger endeavor simply due to its scale. On-prem deployment of a Cerebras CS-2/CS-3 involves a large box (it looks like a fridge) that requires significant power (circuits delivering tens of kW) and cooling (Cerebras uses water cooling in its chassis). Not every data center bay can accommodate that without planning. Network integration is also a point: Cerebras boxes attach to host servers and storage; ensuring high-speed data flow (for feeding training data or receiving inference queries) means using InfiniBand or 100Gb+ Ethernet links. From a software pipeline view, using Cerebras for training might mean altering your workflow — e.g., rather than using your standard distributed training script, you’d use Cerebras’ library to run the training on their machine. This could require changes in your code (though Cerebras tries to keep it minimal). For inference, you might set up the Cerebras system as a dedicated service that applications call into. This is somewhat analogous to how one might treat a GPU cluster, but the difference is it’s a single device with its own scheduling. Scaling out Cerebras (if one is not enough) is also different — you’d use their Swarm technology to connect wafers, which is a new concept to integrate compared to just adding more GPU servers behind a load balancer. Another consideration: maintenance and support — a wafer-scale engine is cutting-edge, so having a strong support contract with Cerebras is essential to handle any hardware glitches, which your team likely can’t fix on their own. In summary, integrating Cerebras requires careful facility and workflow planning. It can slot into existing pipelines (especially via their cloud or by using their pre-built model zoo), but it’s a specialized piece of kit that needs targeted effort to adopt. The payoff is tremendous performance, but the path involves more changes than adopting an extra GPU box.
Nvidia GPUs: Integration of Nvidia GPUs is usually the path of least resistance. All major deep learning pipelines are already built around GPUs. If you have a server with available PCIe slots or an accelerator chassis, adding a GPU is plug-and-play. In cloud, spinning up more GPU instances is straightforward. Challenges with GPUs tend to be about scale and deployment, not compatibility. For instance, running a training job on 100 GPUs requires managing distributed tr,aining software, but that’s a known quantity with many examples (Horovod, DDP in PyTorch, etc.). Deploying GPU inference at scale might involve using Kubernetes with GPU support or specialized serving frameworks, but again, the ecosystem supports that readily (Nvidia’s Triton Inference Server is one tool designed exactly for multi-GPU serving). The main integration challenge might be supply and cost — getting enough GPUs in the current market can be tough, which is partly why alternatives are considered. But from a pure integration standpoint, most ML engineers are deeply familiar with GPUs, so there’s little retraining needed. Monitoring and logging tools are built for GPUs, and so on. Essentially, GPUs are the default — integration is a non-issue if you’re already doing AI; the issues are more about how to efficiently utilize them or tune them. This is where new hardware tries to differentiate, but it comes at the cost of integration effort as noted above.
Broadcom ASICs (Hyperscaler chips): For most external organizations, you integrate with these via cloud services (e.g., use Google Cloud TPUs or AWS Trainium instances). Integration then is a matter of adapting your software to those platforms. Using TPUs on GCP requires using Google’s tools (which might mean converting PyTorch code to TensorFlow if not already). That can be a significant overhaul — for example, if your training code is PyTorch-based and you want to use TPU v4 pods, you essentially have to rewrite in JAX or TensorFlow and ensure all ops are supported by XLA. This is a non-trivial integration effort and often a deal-breaker unless the cost/performance gains are so large that it justifies it. On AWS, using Trainium is easier for PyTorch users (Neuron SDK can compile many models with minimal changes), but there’s still a step of compilation that you need to incorporate into your pipeline (maybe in your model deployment scripts). On-prem ASICs are rare — only the owner companies use them internally (like Meta’s in-house accelerators, Google’s TPUs in Google data centers). If you were, say, a partner university getting a TPU cluster from Google, you’d have to integrate it similarly to how you would on cloud, using Google’s software stack. In any case, because each ASIC has its own “way of doing things,” integration means learning new tools and ensuring your model is compatible. There can also be hardware-level integration concerns: e.g., TPUs might need certain network setup to feed data at full speed, or specific storage solutions to keep the ASIC busy (since if the data pipeline is slow, the ASIC sits idle — an integration pitfall). Moreover, these ASICs typically exist alongside GPUs in workflows (not entirely replacing them), so teams end up maintaining two codebases: one for GPUs and one for TPUs, for instance, which is an ongoing cost. This contrasts with sticking to one universal platform (GPU) for all stages. Hence, the fragmentation and maintenance burden can be an integration challenge with ASICs (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News) (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News).
Bottom Line: SambaNova, Groq, and Cerebras offer compelling technical advantages in throughput, efficiency, and memory for large AI models, but they each come with integration considerations in terms of ecosystem and workflow changes. Nvidia’s GPUs and Broadcom-backed ASICs (like TPUs) represent the current mainstream and hyperscaler approaches, respectively, with GPUs providing broad flexibility/ease and ASICs offering targeted efficiency at the cost of some flexibility and higher upfront development (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News) (Broadcom Takes on Nvidia: The Battle for AI Accelerator Supremacy Heats Up | AI News). The best choice depends on the specific use-case: massive scale deployments might justify new architectures for cost and performance gains, whereas smaller or rapidly evolving use-cases might favor the maturity and versatility of GPUs. All these options continue to evolve, so the landscape in both technology and integration will need reassessment with each generation of hardware.
