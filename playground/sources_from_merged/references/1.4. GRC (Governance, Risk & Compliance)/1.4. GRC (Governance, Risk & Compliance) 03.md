It first explores why these are necessary as a means of transferring rights to a software project before examining in detail the different types of CLA, the problems of CLAs and the rationale behind the newer DCO. Understanding Copyright The article on Licenses explains copyright in the following way: By default, in many jurisdictions (e.g. the US) the author of software holds the copyright to it, granting them exclusive rights to the work. Wikipedia Open source software is often the result of the combined efforts of many contributors who each have copyright over their own contributions. The CLA / DCO works in a similar way to a software license in order to grant rights from the copyright holder to another entity: A software license is issued by the maintainer of some software, granting an end user some rights over a piece of software (such as allowing them to use it). Similarly, the CLA or DCO is issued by the contributor of some code, granting the maintainer the right to license the contribution as part of the main project. The purpose of a CLA is to ensure that the guardian of a project's outputs has the necessary ownership or grants of rights over all contributions to allow them to distribute under the chosen license, often by granting an irrevocable license to allow the project maintainer to use the contribution - Contributor License Agreement, Wikipedia CLA Rationale CLAs were introduced as a way to manage the legal issues that arise when software projects accept contributions from external contributors. Before the widespread use of CLAs, contributors often granted implicit copyright licenses to project maintainers when they submitted their code. This created uncertainty about the ownership and licensing of the code, and made it difficult to incorporate contributions into the project. By using a CLA, project maintainers can establish clear rules about how contributions will be licensed and can ensure that they have the necessary permissions to use and distribute the code. This can help prevent legal disputes and make it easier for projects to grow and evolve over time. For example, after signing the Apache CLA for a project with a matching Apache License the contributor agrees: Unless [the maintainers] explicitly state otherwise, any Contribution intentionally submitted for inclusion in the Work by [the maintainers] to the Licensor shall be under the terms and conditions of this License, without any additional terms or conditions. - Apache License 2.0 Additionally, CLAs can also provide some legal protection to project maintainers by requiring contributors to certify that their contributions do not infringe on anyone else's intellectual property rights. This can help shield the project from liability if a contributor's code turns out to be infringing. For example, the CLA might have a clause saying: You represent that each of Your Contributions is Your original creation ... include[ing] complete details of any third-party license or other restriction ... of which you are personally aware - Apache CLA Popular CLA Management Tools Many open source projects on GitHub have built-in workflows to enforce CLA signing for new contributors, often using one of the following tools: CLA assistant: CLA assistant is a tool that helps organizations automate their CLA signing process. It integrates with GitHub and other code hosting platforms. EasyCLA: EasyCLA is a tool developed by the Linux Foundation. It provides a web-based interface for managing CLAs and allows contributors to sign the CLA electronically. Gerrit: Gerrit is a popular code review tool that also includes CLA management features. Two Types of CLA Copyright In Corporate Environments In general, when developers create software or other original works as part of their job duties, in many jurisdictions the copyright ownership typically belongs to the employer rather than the individual developer. This means that it is the employer that has the right to use, modify, distribute, and profit from the work, not the developer. Note: this is not true in every jurisdiction. Where a contribution is coming from an individual (such as a hobbyist developer) the CLA is considered as an Individual Contributor License Agreement (ICLA). In this case, the individual owns the copyright to their contribution and is able to sign the ICLA. Where a contribution is made from a corporation (such as a developer working within a financial institution) a Corporate Contributor License Agreement (CCLA) is often required. In this case, the copyright is owned by the corporation and the entity must assign a CLA Manager to sign off CCLAs on behalf of the corporation. The CLA Manager will need to maintain the list (a "Schedule A") of all the employees in the organisation covered by CCLAs, likely on a per-project basis. Issues For CLAs Depending on the wording of the CLA and the license, the CLA usually may grant the project irrevocable copyright to use the contribution. This has caused controversy with open source projects in the past: When a CLA requires a contributor to assign unrestricted republishing rights to the project, contributed code can be relicensed at the discretion of the project, even when the CLA does not assign copyright to the project. Prominent open source advocates regard CLAs as dangerous to open source rights ... In 2019 MongoDB used these rights granted by its CLA to achieve a move to a non-open-source license. Contributor License Agreement, Wikipedia Individuals may make contributions on behalf of their corporate employers or on their own behalf. It might not be clear which. Some organisations deal with this issue by asking their employees to have separate GitHub accounts for their different activities. Since individuals join and leave organisations, it becomes necessary for either the project or the CLA tool to keep track of who is or isn't under a CLA at the time of contributing. For example, if an individual makes a corporate contribution (with signed CCLA) one day, then leaves the organisation and makes an individual contribution the next day, the CCLA will no longer be in force for this contribution and technically the individual may be able to assert copyright over the latter contribution. It is therefore important that the CLA Manager is kept up-to-date with employees leaving the organisation. For this reason, many organisations (such as Apache Software Foundation) demand an ICLA for every contributor. As far as Apache are concerned, the CCLA is for the contributor's own protection. This may mean contributors have to process both an ICLA and CCLA: The CCLA is a backup document that the committer/ICLA signer may use to eliminate all of the ambiguity between conflicting laws, contracts, policies and job assignments. We've never required it. Many committers are confident of their individual representations under the ICLA, many other committers find it reassuring that their company has backed up their own ICLA with this umbrella document. It is the ICLA signatory's call if it is required, but it isn't exactly an easy call for many committers employed in the IT/Software industry. Apache CLA FAQ Because CCLAs are a legal document, the organisation's legal team will likely need to be involved in the sign off process. As the terms in each CCLA might be different, this might place a burden on the legal team where multiple projects are involved. Developer Certificate of Origin Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO) was invented as a way to give credit and ownership to developers for their contributions to open source projects while also clarifying the intellectual property rights and licensing of those contributions. The DCO was created by the Linux Foundation, as a way to ensure that all contributions to the Linux kernel were properly licensed and that there was a clear record of who had contributed to the project. The DCO requires developers to sign off each of their commits, indicating that they have the right to contribute the code and that they are licensing their contribution under the project's chosen open source license. DCO Tooling GitHub's DCO App. This app enforces the DCO on Pull Requests. It requires all commit messages to contain the Signed-off-by line with an email address that matches the commit author. Git's Sign-off Feature. Allows developers to sign their commits. CLA vs DCO CLAs are typically used by larger projects and organizations that have more complex legal needs, whereas DCO is a simpler and more lightweight alternative to a CLA. The main difference between CLA and DCO is the level of legal formality involved: CLAs are more formal and legally binding, while DCOs are more informal and rely on the community's trust and good faith. CLAs require contributors to sign a legal agreement, which can be a barrier to entry for some contributors. DCOs, on the other hand, require only a simple sign-off line, which is easier for contributors to understand and comply with. So, DCOs are "easier", but they are not without risk: if it turns out a developer in fact did not have permission to contribute (or may just didn't understand what they were signing), and then contributed something they should not have, there could be complex and expensive unwinding of the contribution that would need to occur, that could even lead to litigation. Organizations using DCO may want to track which of their developers have made contributions via a DCO for compliance and audit reasons. Ultimately, the choice between a CLA and a DCO depends on the needs of the project and its contributors. Further Reading Should I Use a Developer's Certificate of Origin or a Contributor Agreement? All You Need to Know About Open Source License Compliance Platform Solutions Resources Company Get Started Get Started Get Started Software Supply Chain Security All You Need to Know About Open Source License Compliance Discover common misconceptions about open-source licenses and learn best practices for compliance to avoid legal, financial, and operational risks. Finite State Team September 5, 2024 Open-source licenses permit users to use, modify, and distribute software under specific terms and conditions. The licenses ensure the software's source code is available to the public, fostering a collaborative environment. However, the freedom of open-source software can also lead to confusion and misunderstandings about its usage and legal restrictions. Before we look at non-compliance risks, let's look at some of the most common misunderstandings about open-source licenses in more detail. Common misunderstandings about open source licenses All open source licenses are the same There are multiple open-source licenses, each with its own restrictions and requirements. Learn more about license characteristics in our complete guide to open source licenses. Open source means free to use without restrictions One of the most common misconceptions is that open source = free to use without restrictions. This is not the case. While most open source software is free to use, all have licenses that dictate usage, modification, and distribution rules. Ignoring these restrictions can lead to serious legal consequences that impact the project and the organization. Open source licenses are not enforceable Free-to-use software licenses can't be enforced, right? Wrong! Open source licenses are still legally binding agreements; failure to comply can (and does) result in legal action and monetary damages. Attribution of licenses is not necessary While with some licenses, this is technically true, it's good practice to give credit to the original authors of the software whether the license requires it or not. 7 challenges of open source license compliance & their solutions Identifying open source components Given the number of dependencies in open source code, it can be challenging to identify all the components used in a project. This is particularly true if there are nested dependencies. Solution: Utilize automated tools like Finite State to carry out software composition analysis. These tools identify and track open source components, including their licenses, within your project, so you're aware of what's in use. Understanding license terms Terms and requirements for open source licenses vary along with their complexity. Often, it is challenging to understand the implications of different licenses, especially for developers without legal expertise. Solution: Organizations should prioritize education, providing developers with license templates, FAQs, and guidance documents. Consulting legal experts for assistance in interpreting specific license terms is also helpful. Managing license compatibility Combining code from projects with different licenses can lead to license conflicts and compatibility issues. Solution: Use tools to assess license compatibility, such as SPDX license identifiers and compatibility matrices. Tracking license obligations Open source licenses often have specific obligations, e.g., providing attribution, distributing source code, and preserving copyright notices. Solution: Implement processes and tools for tracking license obligations, such as license headers, notices, and documentation templates. Create checklists or workflows to ensure obligations are addressed before releasing software. Managing forks and modifications Making modifications or creating forks of open source projects can complicate license compliance. This is especially true if the modifications are distributed or the project's license changes over time. Solution: Keep detailed records of modifications and contributions, including changes made, contributors involved, and license implications. Monitor upstream changes and license updates to ensure ongoing compliance with the latest versions of open-source projects. Enforcing compliance across organizations Ensuring consistent compliance with open-source licenses across distributed teams and organizations can be challenging, especially in large-scale projects. Solution: Establish clear policies, procedures, and guidelines for open source license compliance with your organization. Developers should receive training and support to understand their responsibilities and obligations. Automation and collaboration tools can also be helpful to facilitate communication and enforcement of compliance policies. Dealing with license violations Despite following open source license best practices, inadvertent license violations may occur due to oversight, misunderstandings, or changes in dependencies. Addressing these violations can be time-consuming and resource-intensive. Solution: Implement processes to address and rectify license violations quickly. Steps may include removing or replacing non-compliant components, releasing updated software versions with corrected licenses, and communicating transparently with affected parties. The risks of non-compliance with open source licenses Non-compliance with open-source licenses can have serious legal, financial, reputation, and operational consequences. These include: Legal consequences One of the most significant risks of non-compliance is legal action. Failure to comply with these legally binding agreements can lead to lawsuits, injunctions, and monetary damages. Legal proceedings can be costly, time-consuming, and severely damaging to an individual's or organization's reputation. Loss of intellectual property rights Some open-source licenses have provisions dictating how software can be used, modified, and distributed. Failure to comply with these conditions can lead to forfeiture of rights, potentially rendering the software unusable. Reputational damage News of non-compliance spreads quickly, damaging reputations and leading to a loss of credibility. Given the collaborative nature of open-source software, reputation is crucial, and rebuilding the lost trust can be time-consuming and costly. Financial penalties Colligo estimates that the average cost of non-compliance is $14.82 million. Financial penalties for non-compliance quickly add up, especially in the case of litigation when legal fees are added to the mix. Disruption of business operations Legal disputes and other consequences of non-compliance can significantly disrupt operations. These disruptions lead to loss of revenue, productivity, and customer trust. In severe cases, non-compliance may force organizations to cease using or distributing the affected software, affecting product development and service delivery. Exposure to security vulnerabilities Lastly, non-compliance with open source licenses can stop organizations from receiving important security updates and patches. Without these updates, the system is vulnerable to exploitation by malicious actors, increasing the risk of data breaches, system compromises, and other security incidents that come with their own financial and legal headaches. Open source license compliance best practices Understand open source licenses — Familiarize yourself with the different open source license types, including their terms, conditions, and obligations. Pay particular attention to attribution, redistribution, and modification requirements. Establish policies and procedures — Develop clear policies and procedures for managing open source software. Outline responsibilities, approval processes, and guidelines for using, contributing to, and releasing open source code. Keep a detailed inventory of open source components — Maintain an inventory of all open source components used in your projects. (This includes dependencies and their respective licenses.) Utilize tools like Finite State to automate the tracking and management. Review and approve third-party components — Conduct thorough reviews of third-party open-source components before integrating them into projects. Ensure they comply with your organization's policies and project requirements and obtain the necessary approvals from legal and compliance teams. Monitor license compliance regularly — Use tools to analyze code repositories, identify license violations, and track changes to open source components over time. Document license information and make it accessible to developers — Track the license information for your project's open source components, including license text, copyright notices, and any modifications made to the original code. Collaborate with legal experts — Consider engaging legal counsel or seeking legal advice to address complex compliance issues. This will ensure that your projects adhere to the relevant laws and regulations. Contribute back to the community — If you modify or extend open source software, consider contributing your changes to the community under the same open source license type. This practice helps foster collaboration, transparency, and innovation within the open-source ecosystem. Stay informed — Subscribe to relevant newsletters, join online communities, and participate in conferences and events to stay informed about developments in open-source licensing. Future trends in open source licensing Looking ahead, several key trends are shaping the landscape of open source licensing. The increase in SBOM (software bill of materials) usage is driven by the need for transparency and accountability in software supply chains — particularly with government contracts. This growing widespread adoption of SBOMs will help organizations better understand and manage their dependencies, thereby enhancing security and compliance efforts. We're already seeing an increase in the popularity of permissive licenses, which offer greater freedom to use and distribute software. To go along with this, we will likely see a shift towards more industry-specific approaches to open-source licensing as businesses seek tailored solutions that align with unique regulatory requirements. And finally, adopting standardization efforts is the hot topic of the day. Initiatives like SPDX (Software Package Date Exchange) are helping to establish common frameworks and best practices, facilitating clearer communication and compliance across the open-source ecosystem. Conclusion In conclusion, open-source licenses play a crucial role in fostering collaboration and innovation by granting users the freedom to use, modify, and distribute software. However, navigating the complexities of open-source licensing can be challenging, leading to common misunderstandings and risks of non-compliance. Failure to comply with open-source licenses can have severe legal, financial, reputation, and operational consequences. That is why it is essential to understand license terms, implement clear policies, track license obligations, and consult with legal experts to ensure license compliance at every stage of the project. LINDDUN GO for lean privacy analysis LINDDUN PRO for systematic privacy analysis LINDDUN MAESTRO for model-driven analysis LINDDUN GO takes on a lean, cross-team approach in finding privacy issues. GO comes in the form of a card deck representing the most common privacy threats, with the key hotspots to look for in your system. These self-contained cards will guide you through the privacy assessment. Best performed in a structured brainstorming setting with a diverse team of privacy enthusiasts. LINDDUN PRO takes on a systematic and exhaustive approach in finding privacy issues. Starting point is a DFD system abstraction, where you focus on all interactions between DFD elements and investigate potential privacy threats. Available knowledge support: privacy threat types, privacy threat trees, mapping table. PRO allows you to leverage tooling to automate your analysis activities. LINDDUN MAESTRO takes on a systematic and exhaustive approach in finding privacy issues by leveraging an enriched system description to enable more precise threat elicitation. Starting point is a threat-specific system abstraction, to support the advanced analysis for threats of that particular type. More info coming soon. WHAT IS THREAT MODELING? When threat modeling, we ask 4 basic questions*: What are we working on? Before you can think about what can go wrong, you need to understand the system under analysis. Start with creating a model of the system, i.e. a representation of the system's key elements. What can go wrong? Analyze the system model to identify potential privacy threats. This is where LINDDUN provides help, with its threat types, trees, elicitation methods, and more. What are we going to do about it? Now you need to tackle the identified privacy threats: prioritize them by assessing the risks and address the threats. Did we do a good job? Reflect on your work: reiterate and refine if needed.
