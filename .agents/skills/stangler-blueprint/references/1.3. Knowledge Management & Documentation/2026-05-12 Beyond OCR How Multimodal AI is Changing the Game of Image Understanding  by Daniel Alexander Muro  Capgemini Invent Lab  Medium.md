Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed the way we work with text, powering everything from chatbots to content generation and even complex decision-making processes. They excel at understanding, generating, and refining textual content, making them invaluable tools across various industries. But their capabilities don’t stop at text alone. With the rise of multimodal models — capable of processing and understanding different types of data such as images and audio — new opportunities are emerging. From voice-to-text, videos-to-text, and image-to-text, we are entering a new era of AI applications. One area of growing interest is image-to-text processing, which aims to extract meaningful information from images and convert it into structured text. For a long time, we relied solely on Optical Character Recognition (OCR) to extract text from images, a method that while useful, often struggled with complex layouts, diagrams, and contextual understanding. However, the emergence of multimodal models has changed the game. These models can analyze both visual and textual elements simultaneously, offering a more comprehensive approach to information extraction. This is especially important in the context of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), a technique that enhances LLMs by integrating external sources of information to improve their responses. Until now, RAG systems have mostly relied on text data, using OCR to pull content from images. But with the development of multimodal models, it’s worth asking: Is OCR still enough, or can these models provide deeper, more accurate insights? To explore this, we set up an evaluation to compare state-of-the-art models like Pixtral, GPT-4o, and Gemini. Using a series of structured metrics, we aimed to measure how well they extract and organize information from images, and whether they truly outperform traditional OCR methods in practical applications.
From PowerPoint to Images: Understanding the Data Source The goal isn’t just to extract plain text from PowerPoint slides; it’s about interpreting and analyzing all the visual elements within them. PowerPoint slides are more than just collections of text; they are structured visual compositions designed to communicate information through a mix of text, visuals, and layout. Titles, subtitles, and bullet points follow a structured hierarchy, while graphical elements like charts, graphs, and images often contain critical insights that go beyond plain text. Extracting meaningful information requires more than just OCR — it demands an understanding of how these elements interact. Text placement, font size, and color indicate importance, while spatial relationships between elements provide context. A pie chart, an image, symbol or logo, for instance, may hold key information that traditional text-based approaches overlook. Additionally, some slides present content in non-linear ways, making structured extraction even more complex and requiring a more flexible approach to extract meaningful insights. Given these complexities, a multimodal approach is essential for accurately interpreting slide content. By analyzing both textual and visual components, these models enable a deeper understanding of the information presented, leading to more precise extraction and improved downstream applications, such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems.
Understanding Multimodal models Multimodal models are advanced machine learning systems that process and integrate multiple data types, such as text, images, audio, and video. Unlike single-modality models, they combine different data streams to offer a deeper and more comprehensive understanding. These models are particularly useful for tasks requiring visual comprehension, such as document analysis, chart interpretation, and diagram data extraction, making them highly effective for extracting and contextualizing information from images. Some of the key multimodal models currently excelling in image information extraction include: Open-Source Models: CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining, January 2021): Developed by OpenAI, CLIP learns to associate images with textual descriptions by training on vast image-text datasets, making it highly effective for tasks like image classification and object recognition. FLAVA by Facebook AI (November 2021): Combining vision and language, FLAVA excels in document layout analysis and extracting visual elements associated with textual content. BLIP (Bootstrapping Language-Image Pre-training, February 2022): Well-suited for vision-language tasks, BLIP is particularly effective at generating detailed captions and extracting structured data from images. Proprietary API-Based Models: GPT-4 (Vision-Enabled, March 2023): Developed by OpenAI, GPT-4 excels at analyzing both text and visual content, making it highly effective for tasks like document reading, graph interpretation, and text extraction from images. Gemini 1.5 by Google DeepMind (February 2024): A cutting-edge multimodal model optimized for extracting structured information from complex visuals, such as charts, tables, and technical diagrams. Pixtral 12b and Pixtral Large (September 2024, November 2024): Designed for detailed image analysis, these models specialize in accurately identifying and extracting specific visual and textual elements.

Evaluation Metrics Evaluating generated text is always a challenge, particularly when working with multimodal data that combines textual and visual elements. To ensure the effectiveness of our approach, we’ve established several metrics that assess various key aspects of the extracted information. These metrics allow us to systematically evaluate both the accuracy and efficiency of our system, ensuring it meets real-world needs. 5.1. Fact Recall: Measuring Completeness Recall is a critical metric for evaluating how much of the relevant information from the slides is successfully extracted. It focuses on identifying any missing information that should have been captured. Recall checks if the model has extracted all the necessary information, verifying whether any important data was omitted or left unextracted. If the model fails to extract certain key elements, recall will highlight those gaps. High recall means that the system is thorough and captures most, if not all, of the expected data. However, improving recall often involves balancing other factors, such as precision, which may be affected if irrelevant details are included during extraction. To measure recall, we followed a systematic approach: Step 1: Creating the Ground Truth We began by manually extracting a comprehensive list of facts from each image. These facts served as the ground truth, encapsulating all the critical details conveyed by the slide. Step 2: Comparison and Critical Evaluation Next, we utilized a secondary LLM to compare the manually annotated atomic facts with the textual output generated by the primary LLM during its analysis. This process involved a critical evaluation of each atomic fact to determine whether it was present or absent in the extracted output. The comparison was conducted with a focus on precision, ensuring that the system could reliably identify gaps in the analysis. Step 3: Calculating Recall To quantify the completeness of the information extraction process, we calculated recall by dividing the total number of correctly identified atomic facts by the total number of atomic facts in the ground truth. This metric provided a clear measure of the system’s ability to comprehensively capture relevant information from the slides. 5.2. Precision: Ensuring Accuracy Precision evaluates the correctness of the extracted information by measuring the proportion of extracted content that is accurate. This metric is crucial for minimizing errors, as even a small amount of incorrect information can undermine the system’s reliability. Precision focuses on the accuracy of the extracted content, ensuring that the model does not hallucinate irrelevant or erroneous details. It quantifies how much of the extracted information is actually correct and relevant, and how often the model might introduce false positives or misleading results. This metric is crucial because extracting incorrect labels from a graph or misinterpreting a symbol can lead to significant misunderstandings in the downstream analysis. To measure precision, we followed a slightly similar approach: Step 1: Extracting Atomic Facts The initial step involved tasking an LLM with extracting atomic facts from the analysis output. An atomic fact is defined as a minimal, self-contained unit of information that cannot be further decomposed without losing its meaning. These atomic facts encapsulate the essential pieces of information identified by the model during the information extraction phase. Step 2: Verification Against the Slide Image Subsequently, a second LLM was utilized to cross-verify each extracted atomic fact against the corresponding slide image. This step ensured that the extracted information accurately reflected the visual content and context of the slide. Step 3: Calculating Precision Precision was then computed by dividing the number of correctly verified atomic facts by the total number of atomic facts extracted. This ratio provided a clear metric for assessing the accuracy and relevance of the extracted information. 5.3. Grammatical Accuracy: Evaluating Content Quality Beyond recall and precision, the quality of the generated text matters greatly, especially for end-users. Texts with poor grammar can hinder readability, misrepresent the intended meaning, and reduce the perceived reliability of the system. To measure grammatical accuracy, we tasked an LLM with systematically accomplishing the following steps: Step 1: Error Identification The extracted text was analyzed looking for grammatical errors. The evaluation focused on key aspects of grammar, including: Subject-verb agreement: Ensuring that subjects and verbs are correctly paired in number and person. Tense consistency: Verifying that verb tenses are used appropriately and consistently. Sentence structure: Checking for proper construction of sentences, avoiding fragments or run-ons. Article usage: Ensuring correct usage of definite and indefinite articles. Punctuation: Identifying missing or incorrect punctuation marks. Word choice: Detecting inappropriate or ambiguous word usage. The LLM flagged and counted the number of grammatical errors present in the text. This provided a quantitative measure of linguistic accuracy. Cost and Time Efficiency Beyond accuracy and quality, practical factors like cost and time efficiency are critical, especially when processing large numbers of images. Model Comparison: Which One Performs Best? Our evaluation of model performance across recall and precision metrics reveals clear differences in how well each model extracts information. Gemini 1.5 Pro stands out as the best overall performer, achieving the highest recall (76%) while maintaining excellent precision (99%), making it a well-balanced option for accurate and comprehensive information retrieval. GPT 4o follows closely, with a recall of 75% and a strong precision of 94%, indicating it effectively captures relevant details with minimal errors. GPT 4o Mini excels in precision 99%, but its recall 67% suggests it may miss some details while ensuring high accuracy in what it does extract. Pixtral Large and Gemini 1.5 Flash offer a solid balance between recall and precision, while Pixtral 12b performs slightly lower across both metrics. On the other hand, the OCR model falls significantly behind, with a recall of just 26% and a precision of 61%, highlighting its limitations in handling the complexity of the slides compared to the more advanced multimodal models. When evaluating the grammatical accuracy of the extracted content, Gemini 1.5 Pro demonstrated the highest performance, achieving a score of 4, indicating only minor errors that do not affect readability. Similarly, GPT 40 Mini followed closely with a score of 4, while GPT 40 scored slightly lower at 3.86, still reflecting a strong grasp of grammatical accuracy. Pixtral Large and Pixtral 12b both received scores of 4.14, positioning them above GPT 40 but slightly below Gemini 1.5 Pro. Meanwhile, Gemini 1.5 Flash received a score of 4.29, showcasing a strong performance in grammatical consistency. On the other hand, the OCR model exhibited the weakest grammatical performance, scoring 2.43, which indicates significant errors that frequently impact readability. These results suggest that while advanced LLMs maintain a high level of grammatical precision, traditional OCR systems struggle with producing grammatically accurate outputs. The analysis also emphasizes the clear advantage of multimodal AI models over traditional OCR approaches for extracting meaningful insights from PowerPoint slides. Finally, when evaluating the feasibility of deploying multimodal models at scale, both cost efficiency and execution time are critical factors. Our analysis of processing 100,000 images reveals significant differences across the models in terms of pricing and performance. In terms of cost, Gemini 1.5 Flash stands out as the most cost-effective option at less than $20, followed by GPT-4o Mini at $40 and Pixtral 12B at around $50.00. On the higher end of the spectrum, Gemini 1.5 Pro comes at a considerably higher cost of $250, while GPT-4o and Pixtral Large are the most expensive options, costing $680 and $680, respectively. Regarding execution time, Gemini 1.5 Flash also proves to be the fastest, with an average processing time of 6.38 seconds per image, closely followed by Gemini 1.5 Pro at 6.61 seconds and Pixtral 12B at 7.14 seconds. The GPT-4o models and Pixtral Large exhibit slightly longer processing times, ranging from 7.18 to 7.57 seconds, with GPT-4o being the slowest among them. The OCR model, however, demonstrates the longest execution time at 12.39 seconds per image, highlighting its slower performance compared to multimodal LLMs. These findings suggest that while models like Gemini 1.5 Flash offer a compelling balance of cost and speed, higher-end models such as Gemini 1.5 Pro and GPT 4o may provide additional capabilities at a significantly higher cost. Choosing the right model depends on the balance between budget constraints, processing speed requirements, and the desired level of information extraction accuracy.
In Summary: Achievements and Outcomes The evaluation of multimodal LLMs for extracting information from PowerPoint slides has revealed key insights into their performance, cost-effectiveness, and processing efficiency. Gemini 1.5 Pro stands out as a top performer, delivering robust capabilities across all evaluated criteria, including text features, semantic understanding, and grammar accuracy. It offers a strong balance between cost and performance, making it an ideal choice for those seeking high accuracy without incurring the substantial costs associated with models like GPT-4o and Pixtral Large. (average price to process 100K images per model) Meanwhile, Gemini 1.5 Flash emerges as the most cost-effective solution, providing solid performance in essential tasks such as text extraction and semantic comprehension at a fraction of the cost of other models. While it may have some limitations in handling complex text structures, it remains a compelling choice for budget-conscious applications. In contrast, GPT-4o and Pixtral Large, despite their comprehensive feature sets and ability to handle advanced tasks such as spatial hierarchy and grid detection, come at a significantly higher cost. Their pricing makes them less attractive for scenarios where a more cost-efficient solution, such as Gemini 1.5 Pro, can achieve similar or even better results. Lastly, GPT-4o Mini and Pixtral 12B offer a middle-ground solution, with moderate pricing and reasonable performance across key areas. They provide good semantic understanding and grammar capabilities but may not be as cost-effective compared to the more affordable and equally capable Gemini models. Overall, the results emphasize the strengths of Gemini 1.5 Pro as the most well-rounded solution, delivering high performance at a reasonable cost, while Gemini 1.5 Flash remains an attractive choice for projects with tighter budgets.
Conclusion While this study provides valuable insights into the performance of various multimodal LLMs in extracting and processing information from PowerPoint slides, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. Our evaluation was conducted on a limited dataset of only seven images, which, even while giving us a good indication of overall trends, may not fully reflect how these models perform across a wider range of document types and complexities. The findings offer a solid foundation for understanding the trade-offs between cost, execution time, and accuracy among different models. However, a more comprehensive analysis with a larger and more diverse dataset is essential to draw definitive conclusions. This would allow for a deeper evaluation of the models’ consistency, scalability, and ability to handle different challenges that may not have been captured in this initial assessment.
