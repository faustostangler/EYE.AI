Versions Notes Article Views Citations - Abstract With artificial intelligence (AI) rapidly increasing in popularity and presence in everyday life, new applications utilizing AI are being explored across virtually all domains, from banking and healthcare to cybersecurity to generative AI for images, voice, and video content creation. With that trend comes an inherent need for increased AI capabilities. One cornerstone of AI applications is the ability of generative AI to consume documents and utilize their content to answer questions, generate new content, correlate it with other data sources, and more. No longer constrained to text alone, we now leverage multimodal AI models to help us understand visual elements within documents, such as images, tables, figures, and charts. Within this realm, capabilities have expanded exponentially from traditional Optical Character Recognition (OCR) approaches towards increasingly utilizing complex AI models for visual content analysis and understanding. Modern approaches, especially those leveraging AI, are now focusing on interpreting more complex diagrams such as flowcharts, block diagrams, Unified Modeling Language (UML) diagrams, electrical schematics, and timing diagrams. These diagram types combine text, symbols, and structured layout, making them challenging to parse and comprehend using conventional techniques. This paper presents a historical analysis and comprehensive survey of scientific literature exploring this domain of visual understanding of complex technical illustrations and diagrams. We explore the use of deep learning models, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and transformer-based architectures. These models, along with OCR, enable the extraction of both textual and structural information from visually complex sources. Despite these advancements, numerous challenges remain, however. These range from hallucinations, where the content extraction system produces outputs not grounded in the source image, which leads to misinterpretations, to a lack of contextual understanding of diagrammatic elements, such as arrows, grouping, and spatial hierarchy. This survey focuses on five key diagram types: flowcharts, block diagrams, UML diagrams, electrical schematics, and timing diagrams. It evaluates the effectiveness, limitations, and practical solutions—both traditional and AI-driven—that aim to enable the extraction of accurate and meaningful information from complex diagrams in a way that is trustworthy and suitable for real-world, high-accuracy AI applications. This survey reveals that virtually all approaches struggle with accurately extracting technical diagram information. It also illustrates a path forward. Pursuing research to further improve their accuracy is crucial for supporting and enabling various applications, including complex document question answering and Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG), document-driven AI agents, accessibility applications, and automation. Keywords: Visual Image Understanding; technical illustrations; document understanding; deep learning; Optical Character Recognition; structural context analysis; flowchart; block diagram; UML diagram; electrical schematic; timing diagram
Introduction We are experiencing the rapid proliferation of AI in our everyday lives. We can now find AI applications in virtually every domain, from virtual assistants and AI researchers to cybersecurity tools, intelligent industrial automation systems, and much more. Driven by generative AI, these tools are increasingly multi-modal. They are no longer constrained to text and can be used to generate images, videos, sounds, and voices. They can also be used to analyze multi-modal sources, such as comprehending text, summarizing image content, and intelligently editing videos. In particular, the ability to extract meaningful information from visual data has long been a central focus of computer vision research, dating back to traditional algorithmic OCR methods. Initially, OCR was developed to recognize typed or handwritten text in scanned documents, converting them from pixel-based images into machine-readable formats, such as plain text, source code, and formatted text. Robotics applications have pushed the boundaries of what computer vision algorithms can achieve, but they have always been limited by the capabilities of the underlying algorithms. In recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence and machine learning has led to significant advancements across many fields, including image content analysis. While traditional OCR was limited to structured, text-heavy inputs, such as books, forms, or printed documents, modern deep learning techniques have been introduced that can support more complex visual data. One particular emerging application domain for AI image analysis is the automation of extracting complex descriptions of both text and structure from technical diagrams and other technical illustrations. These types of illustrations are commonly found in engineering, software development, and electronics, and contain a mixture of textual, symbolic, and graphical elements. Increasingly, AI models are being used to extract not only the textual content but also vital structural and relational information from these diagrams, enabling greater automation, analysis, and digital transformation. This capability is critical for applications such as RAG [ 1], where large language models (LLMs) are used together with information extracted from documents to answer specific questions related to these documents. For any RAG pipeline, it is critically important to capture all content of the provided documents, not just their textual aspects. It also greatly aids in question-answering processes, enabling the generation of full and accurate answers to user questions by extracting relevant text and visual information from technical documents. This also supports accessibility applications, such as screen readers, in accurately describing visual content, as well as many sophisticated and AI-driven automation applications. Thus, the ability to extract visual details from technical illustrations within these documents, alongside the textual content, is crucial for accurately answering user questions, informed decision-making, and more. This image content extraction survey focuses on five specific, widely used types of technical illustrations: flowcharts, block diagrams, UML diagrams, electrical schematics, and timing diagrams. For analysis purposes, block diagrams and UML diagrams are grouped together due to their shared structural characteristics and hierarchical representation approaches. These diagram types are important elements in technical documentation, user guides, network protocol specifications, software and algorithm documentations, and many other types of documents crucial to virtually all scientific fields and end-user applications. Our aim for this survey is to provide an overview of the current state-of-the-art in image content analysis for technical illustrations, including their current capabilities, shortcomings, and suggested areas of further research. From a conceptual standpoint, many diagram-understanding approaches can be viewed as structured prediction problems, where an input image is mapped to a structured semantic representation, such as a graph, netlist, program, or symbolic description. In practice, however, the surveyed literature adopts a wide variety of task-specific representations and intermediate formats, reflecting differing application goals rather than a unified formalism. Accordingly, this survey does not impose a single mathematical definition of diagram understanding, but instead evaluates methods based on the structure, fidelity, and usability of their extracted representations. Flowcharts and block diagrams excel at representing complex systems in a clear and easy-to-understand format. They can be used to describe processes, systems, data flow, computer programs and algorithms, document structure, workflows, and decision-making. In addition, closely related to flowcharts are State Machine Diagrams and more structured forms such as swimlane flowcharts. These diagram types are widely used in fields such as engineering, electronics, and telecommunications, where systems need to be broken down into smaller components for analysis, design, and troubleshooting. Figure 1 shows a typical example of a flowchart, and a simple block diagram is shown in Figure 2. Figure 1. Comparison flowchart example. Figure 2. Computer block diagram example. Another category of box-and-connector diagrams widely used in software engineering is UML diagrams. While block diagrams typically represent hardware and system architecture, UML diagrams model software structure and behavior. Despite their different application domains, both diagram types share similar visual characteristics—boxes connected by lines—and thus present comparable challenges for content extraction. From a computer vision perspective, both require detecting rectangular shapes, extracting embedded text, identifying connecting lines and their directionality, and understanding spatial relationships. Figure 3 shows a basic UML diagram. Figure 3. UML vehicle diagram example. When it comes to electrical systems and electronics, scientists and engineers often rely on electrical circuit diagrams, which provide a detailed and standardized visual representation of circuits and electronic components, as well as the connections between them, to achieve a specific function. Circuit diagrams serve as blueprints for electrical systems, converting abstract design concepts into a visual representation that is easy to understand. A circuit diagram that describes a microcontroller (MCU) pin-out is shown in Figure 4. Figure 4. MCU circuit diagram example. Finally, this survey also specifically reviews approaches related to the analysis of timing diagrams. They are a type of visual representation that describes the behavior of signals or events over time, for example, in digital circuits, systems, or communication networks. Timing diagrams provide a clear and organized way of observing how various signals evolve and interact over specific intervals. While there is a wide variety of diagrams that could be loosely grouped into timing diagrams, in this survey, we strictly refer to those describing signal level changes over time as timing diagrams. An example timing diagram is shown in Figure 5, illustrating the relationship between the clock, reset, address, data, write enable, and ready signals. Figure 5. Timing diagram example. These diagram types all pose unique challenges. While OCR can typically be applied to extract the textual content, a comprehensive representation of these diagram types encompasses far more than simply text. It needs to include shapes, color, and the structural relation between all these elements, in order to fully capture their content and meaning. OCR itself has evolved over the years, progressing from a purely algorithmic approach to incorporating various deep learning techniques. Despite these improvements, significant limitations remain. One major challenge arising from the use of AI models is hallucination, where AI models generate text or structures that are not grounded in actual content. Another challenge is the lack of contextual understanding. Many image content analysis systems still struggle to accurately interpret connections such as directional arrows, grouped symbols, or hierarchical structures, all of which are crucial in understanding the intent and logic behind technical diagrams. These issues can lead to flawed outputs, misinterpretations, or even cause system failures when used in automated pipelines. This paper presents a comprehensive survey and historical analysis of image content extraction techniques, including OCR, applied to the five major diagram types mentioned above. It encompasses both traditional and AI-driven approaches, highlighting their methodologies, strengths, and specific limitations, with a focus on AI advances within this area of research. By analyzing a wide range of models and algorithms, we assess their effectiveness in extracting both textual and structural information. We also evaluate their readiness for deployment in high-accuracy, real-world environments. The remainder of this paper will be organized as follows. Section 2 will go over the steps for how this survey was conducted. In Section 3, we present a brief review of the evolution of image content analysis approaches and related evaluation techniques. Section 4 presents our survey findings, analyzing and discussing the various research papers we included for this survey, as well as exploring their inspiration, techniques, and results. Finally, in Section 5 we present our conclusions and suggest future research directions.
Survey Methodology This study employed a systematic literature review methodology inspired by the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [ 2] guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility in identifying, screening, and selecting relevant research papers. The review process consisted of four main stages: identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion. 2.1. Eligibility Criteria Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) published between January 2020 and August 2025; (2) proposed, implemented, or evaluated methods for technical diagram extraction or understanding that process complete diagram images to extract structural, semantic, or relational information at the diagram level rather than addressing individual symbols or text elements in isolation; (3) written in English; and (4) provided sufficient methodological detail, including model architecture, training setup, and datasets, to support assessment of transparency and reproducibility. A limited set of time series diagram papers was also included, though these represent a distinct research domain focused on statistical data visualization rather than technical system specifications. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on isolated sub-tasks without diagram-level integration or non-diagram documents (e.g., natural images or plain text). 2.2. Information Sources and Search Strategy A comprehensive literature search was conducted across six major academic databases on 27 August 2025: IEEE Xplore Digital Library, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect (Elsevier), MDPI Digital Library, arXiv, and Google Scholar. The search strategy employed Boolean combinations of controlled vocabulary and free-text terms organized into three concept groups: (1) Deep learning architectures: “deep learning” OR “neural network” OR “CNN” OR “RNN” OR “LSTM” OR “transformer” OR “autoencoder”; (2) Technical diagrams: “technical diagram” OR “engineering diagram” OR “schematic” OR “flowchart” OR “circuit diagram” OR “architectural diagram” OR “UML diagram”; and (3) Diagram extraction and understanding tasks: “diagram extraction” OR “diagram parsing” OR “diagram recognition” OR “symbol detection” OR “diagram segmentation” OR “structure understanding” OR “relationship inference” OR “diagram interpretation.” The complete search string was adapted to the syntax and indexing rules of each database. No restrictions were imposed on study design or geographic location. In addition, reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify further eligible publications through backward citation tracking. 2.3. Selection Process The study selection process followed a multi-stage screening approach, as illustrated in Figure 6. The initial database search identified a total of 307 records. After removing 77 duplicates, 230 unique papers remained for screening. These records underwent title and abstract screening against the predefined eligibility criteria. The screening process was conducted by a single reviewer to ensure consistency in decision-making. However, while we did our utmost to follow strict objective selection criteria, we acknowledge that our choice to use a single reviewer may also introduce selection bias. Given the broad search strategy, full-text articles were retrieved and examined for all 230 records to accurately assess their relevance to deep learning-based technical diagram extraction and understanding tasks. Figure 6. PRISMA 2020 flow diagram illustrating the systematic literature search and selection process, generated using [ 2]. Following full-text screening, 181 articles were excluded as false positives, primarily because they addressed non-diagram document analysis, natural image processing, or focused on converting text to technical diagrams. The remaining 49 studies were then independently categorized according to the primary diagram type addressed (e.g., flowcharts, circuit diagrams, block diagrams, UML diagrams, and timing diagrams). During the literature review, we identified a comprehensive survey published in 2024 [ 3] that provides an in-depth analysis of deep learning techniques for text and shape extraction from Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&ID). As this diagram category was thoroughly covered in the referenced survey, P&ID-focused studies were excluded from the present review to avoid duplication. Consequently, an additional seven papers were excluded, resulting in a final corpus of 42 core studies. Additionally, six time series diagram studies are briefly discussed for comparative purposes in the larger context of timing diagrams, bringing the total to 48 papers. Table 1 summarizes the distribution of the included studies across different diagram types. Of the 42 core technical diagram papers, flowchart diagrams account for 19 studies (39.58%), followed by electrical schematic diagrams with 14 studies (29.17%). Block and UML diagrams contain seven studies (14.58%), while timing diagrams contain only two studies (4.17%). Additionally, six time series diagram studies (12.50%) are included to acknowledge related temporal visualization research. This distribution indicates that existing deep learning-based diagram extraction research has primarily focused on flowchart and electrical schematic diagrams, while other diagram types have received rather limited attention. Table 1. Distribution of identified literature across diagram types (N = 48). 2.4. Data Extraction and Quality Assessment From each eligible study, the following data elements were systematically extracted: author(s), publication year, deep learning architecture type (e.g., CNN, RNN/LSTM, transformer, graph neural network), target diagram type(s) (e.g., flowchart, block and UML diagram, electrical schematic, timing diagram), diagram analysis task(s) addressed (e.g., detection, segmentation, symbol recognition, text extraction, structural parsing, relationship inference, or end-to-end interpretation), dataset characteristics (synthetic versus real-world diagrams, publicly available versus proprietary datasets), and performance metrics. As our review encompasses both traditional computer vision approaches and modern deep learning methods to support historical analysis, we also documented the primary methodological approach (traditional, hybrid, or deep learning-based) for each study. Quality assessment was conducted in a descriptive manner, focusing on the completeness of methodological reporting rather than on comparative performance evaluation. Specifically, studies were assessed for clarity in problem formulation, adequacy of dataset description, transparency of model architecture and training procedures, and the presence of quantitative evaluation results. No cross-study performance comparison, ranking, or benchmarking was performed. All included studies were treated as independent contributions within their respective diagram types and task categories. The full set of 42 studies that met the inclusion criteria formed the basis for the technical analysis presented in Section 4. 2.5. Synthesis Methods and Bibliometric Analysis A qualitative narrative approach was employed to synthesize the selected literature. Studies were organized according to diagram type and the primary diagram analysis task addressed, with attention to the evolution of methodological approaches from traditional computer vision techniques to modern deep learning methods. Key methodological characteristics and reported outcomes were summarized without attempting quantitative aggregation or comparative evaluation. This approach was adopted due to the heterogeneity of datasets, diagram styles, task definitions, and evaluation protocols across studies. To explore thematic clusters and research trends in the broader literature on technical diagram extraction, an initial bibliometric analysis was conducted using VOSviewer (version 1.6.18) on the title and abstract keywords of 230 retrieved papers. Co-occurrence analysis with a minimum keyword frequency threshold of five occurrences revealed prominent research communities, including work on symbol detection, diagram segmentation, structural parsing, relationship inference, and end-to-end diagram interpretation, as illustrated in Figure 7. This preliminary analysis provided an overview of the landscape and informed the subsequent selection and synthesis of studies for the focused review. Figure 7. Background 3.1. Early Image Content Extraction Approaches Over the years, numerous methods have been developed for extracting textual and visual information from documents and images. Earlier approaches focused heavily on OCR, which aims to detect printed or handwritten text and convert it into machine-readable formats. As image processing improved, shape and pattern recognition methods emerged, allowing systems to detect and recognize shapes, diagrams, and geometric figures. In recent years, the emergence of deep learning methods for computer vision tasks, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), has dramatically improved the accuracy and versatility of text and image content extraction techniques [ 4, 5]. These advancements have enabled the development of more intelligent systems capable of handling complex layouts, handwritten variants, and providing contextual understanding. Traditional OCR techniques are the earliest methods for text extraction from images. They typically follow a multi-step process comprised of [ 4]:
Preprocessing involves noise reduction, binarization, and skew correction to enhance image quality. 2. Segmentation separates text regions from non-text elements, and individual characters or words are isolated. 3. Feature Extraction identifies key characteristics of each symbol, such as edges, corners, and stroke widths. 4. Classification and Recognition compares the features to already trained character models to output machine-readable text. 5. Post-Processing is often utilized by modern OCR systems to correct recognition errors using language models. While OCR is primarily limited to text recognition, research into shape recognition techniques focuses on the detection and extraction of symbols, icons, diagrams, and geometric figures. The steps associated with shape recognition typically involve [ 5]: Edge Detection or Contour Extraction is utilized to identify shape boundaries. Shape Description computes area, perimeter, or moments. Feature Matching uses template matching or feature descriptor techniques. Classification maps the detected shapes to predefined categories. These methods are the cornerstone of content extraction from complex images, which comprise technical diagrams, engineering drawings, flowcharts, and other intricate diagrammatic representations. The advent of deep learning has enabled significant advances in image content extraction, particularly for complex technical imagery. For example, CNNs [ 6] are comprised of three primary types of layers, which are typically stacked to extract and combine features from input data before producing a resulting value. By optimally structuring CNNs using these different layer types, the resulting CNNs excel at a variety of computer vision tasks. The three distinct layer types are: Convolutional layers apply a series of learnable filters or kernels that perform convolutional operations over the input image. These filters detect edges, textures, and color gradients. Pooling layers perform a down-sampling operation on the feature maps, reducing their spatial dimensions while retaining the significant information. Fully Connected (FC) layers operate after the feature maps are flattened into a one-dimensional feature vector. Flattening converts the multidimensional feature maps into a single continuous vector. These layers integrate the extracted features and compute the final prediction. The convolutional capabilities of CNNs make them an ideal foundational approach for processing spatial data such as images. For sequential data, such as text, however, RNNs [ 7] often outperform CNNs by modeling contextual dependencies through the process of remembering and incorporating prior input values. Hybrid versions of these frameworks also exist, such as convolutional recurrent neural networks (CRNNs), which combine CNN feature extraction with RNN sequence modeling, enabling end-to-end image content analysis [ 8]. More recently, transformer-based models emerged as a dominant architecture that enables modern large language models (LLMs) and other advances in deep neural networks. LLMs, as a result, have rapidly become a cornerstone of everyday life use cases for AI applications. Transformers are capable of capturing long-range dependencies and contextual relationships across an entire document. Transformer architectures also include non-textual adaptations such as vision transformers (ViT) [ 9] and TrOCR [ 10]. Transformers can often outperform RNNs in recognizing complex layouts [ 11]. Building on this capability, vision transformers were introduced to apply the transformer paradigm to image analysis. They provide the foundational component for building Vision Language Models (VLM), which are multimodal LLMs capable of generating output based on textual and visual content. The initial step in a VLM typically involves vision encoding using ViT (to represent image data using feature vectors), followed by language encoding (to represent the text such as the user query as feature vectors), followed by a projection and integration step (to align both of these embeddings within a common encoding space combined with a transformer model), and finally the output step that generates the text-based result. While many diagram extraction pipelines implicitly involve uncertainty—arising from noisy images, ambiguous symbols, or OCR errors—most surveyed approaches rely on deterministic inference at inference time. Explicit probabilistic modeling and uncertainty propagation are comparatively rare, largely due to computational complexity and the difficulty of defining tractable joint distributions over heterogeneous diagram components. Bayesian and uncertainty-aware methods have been explored in related vision domains, but remain underrepresented in end-to-end technical diagram understanding systems. These deep learning approaches form the technical foundation for extracting content from the five diagram types examined in this survey: flowcharts, block and UML diagrams, electrical schematics, and timing diagrams. Each of these diagram types presents unique challenges in terms of structural complexity, symbolic notation, and spatial relationships that must be captured for accurate interpretation. 3.2. Existing Evaluation Approaches for Content Extraction Methods Having established the technical foundations of content extraction methods, we now turn to the evaluation frameworks used to assess their performance. With the development of these architectures, it became essential to establish and implement rigorous evaluation and scoring mechanisms to quantitatively and qualitatively assess the performance of each such framework, particularly under challenging conditions such as noisy inputs, complex layouts, as well as dense or overlapping content, in order to determine their robustness, reliability, accuracy, and suitability for real-world applications. There are multiple methods that assess these frameworks, with one prominent example being Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation (ROUGE-1) [ 12]. ROUGE-1 is a metric for evaluating text summarization that measures single-word (uni-gram) overlap between a generated summary and one or more reference summaries. It uses Precision, Recall, and F1-Score to quantify performance. The scores range from 0 to 1, where 0 represents no matching of words and 1 represents perfect matching of words. Another scoring technique used for recognition evaluation is the Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU) scoring [ 13]. BLEU is used for evaluating the quality of the extracted text. The BLEU scoring scale ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 represents the lowest quality and 1 the highest [ 14]. Some variants of BLEU scoring include CodeBLEU [ 15], which specifically targets the evaluation of program code extraction, and BLEURT [ 16], which is based on the bidirectional encoder representation from transformers (BERT) to mimic a more human-like evaluation approach for extractions. It uses the same scoring scale as the original BLEU. BLEU-4 [ 17] is primarily used for evaluating extractions, utilizing a comparison to human translations. BLEU-4 is named thus because it utilizes four words to calculate precision, using a scoring scale that once again ranges from 0 to 1. Another scoring approach is the mean Average Precision (mAP) [ 18], which evaluates both the localization and classification performance. While precision is employed to measure the accuracy of the model's positive predictions, recall measures the completeness of the model's predictions, and the F1-score represents the mean of precision and recall [ 19]. For object detection and segmentation tasks, IoU [ 20] is commonly used to measure the overlap between predicted and ground truth bounding boxes or regions. IoU is calculated as the area of overlap divided by the area of union between the prediction and ground truth, producing a score between 0 and 1, where higher values indicate better spatial alignment. IoU is often used in conjunction with mAP, where a prediction is considered correct if its IoU exceeds a specified threshold of 0.5 or 0.75. It is important to note the limitations of cross-study comparisons in this context. As shown above, the various studies employ different evaluation metrics, including BLEU, IoU, mAP, accuracy, ROUGE-1, and F1-score, each of which captures distinct aspects of model performance. Because these metrics measure performance in fundamentally different ways, direct numerical comparison across studies is challenging and may be misleading. Additionally, for ease of interpretation and comparison throughout this survey, certain metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE-1 are reported on a 0–100 scale (obtained by multiplying the original 0–1 scores by 100), though readers should note that source papers typically report these metrics on the original 0–1 scale. While some approaches attempt to normalize metrics to a common scale, such transformations introduce additional assumptions and are not always feasible. Therefore, comparisons across studies should be interpreted qualitatively, focusing on overall trends and methodological differences rather than a direct metric-to-metric evaluation. 4. Review of Identified Papers In 2020, the authors of [ 21] proposed using region-based segmentation to convert flowcharts into graph structures, introducing a custom approach for the precise extraction of 2D elements from flowcharts. They reviewed prior work, such as [ 22], which focused on flowchart extraction from digital images using a neural network, and [ 23], which developed Flow2Code, a mobile app that converted photographs of hand-drawn flowcharts into executable code. While Flow2Code's original text extraction was performed using offline sketch recognition and computer vision, to advance flowchart text extraction for increased precision and structure recovery, the authors of [ 21] proposed and evaluated their own technique. The authors followed a traditional preprocessing step using Gaussian blur for grayscale, Otsu method [ 24] for image thresholding, and thinning was applied to reduce the image to a single-pixel width. Feature extraction was achieved through region-based segmentation, which separates text and non-text regions, also referred to as regions of interest (ROIs) [ 25]. Morphological labeling was then used to extract text from corresponding regions, and shape identification was performed by analyzing contour vertices to determine flowchart shapes. The flowcharts were ultimately converted into graph-like structures composed of nodes, labels, shapes, and edges. A final plot was generated to visualize the graph. The Open Source Computer Vision Library (OpenCV) [ 26] and Python were used for implementation, and their experimental evaluation involved a small dataset of only 25 different flowcharts, which were categorized and converted into graph representations. Each element of the flowcharts was parsed and examined. The approach successfully handled more complex parameters such as scaling, dashed or directed lines, and colored shapes, and achieved 90% accuracy during testing. The content extraction process effectively transformed flowcharts into structured graphs. This result demonstrated the effectiveness of the method in handling diverse graphical features. The authors noted that future work would address greater flowchart complexity and explore alternative extraction methods. The authors of [ 27] created their own method of flowchart extraction called graph recognition convolutional neural network (GRCNN) in 2020. The work is motivated by program synthesis techniques, such as FlashFill [ 28], an algorithm famously used in Microsoft Excel, which takes a set of given input-output text pairs and determines the user-desired text transformation from those samples. Unlike FlashFill's example-based approach, GRCNN uses flowcharts as specifications for program synthesis. In GRCNN, a CNN is utilized for image content extraction, with an edge network used to transform the feature vector from the CNN into an edge description of the given flowchart. Using this approach, the nodes of the flowchart are identified and also described. A custom dataset comprising 2490 flowchart images was used for the evaluation of GRCNN, featuring flowcharts with 3 to 6 nodes and 0 to 2 decisions. These flowcharts were created using Graphviz [ 29]. The edge and sequence scores were 94.1% and 90.6%, respectively. However, for node extraction, a relatively low accuracy score of 67.9% was achieved. Consequently, the overall graph extraction accuracy (comprised of edge, sequence, and node extraction) was only 66.4%. This indicated that significantly more research was needed to consistently and accurately extract the structure of a flowchart. Another approach to flowchart extraction was published in 2020 in [ 30]. It aims to improve upon other flowchart extraction approaches, such as [ 31, 32], by facilitating the extraction of handwritten flowcharts instead of programmatically generated flowcharts. Images are first acquired using a camera or scanner. It then utilizes the Otsu method to transform each input image via threshold-based binarization, followed by morphological operations (erosion and dilation) and application of the Ramer–Douglas–Peucker (RDP) method [ 33] for object and line detection, as well as beam search [ 34] for the handwritten text detection. For evaluating their approach, a small dataset of 20 flowcharts was used. The average word and character accuracies are 66.01% and 86.52%, respectively. While the resulting scores are relatively low, they nonetheless illustrated the potential of this technique and warranted further exploration. The authors themselves stated that their future work aimed to improve the accuracy of flowcharts containing loops and to create more diverse and complex flowcharts. Another study conducted and published in 2020 utilized deep learning and OCR to convert flowcharts into graphs [ 35]. The authors referenced earlier works, such as [ 36], which employed graph grammar for flowchart analysis, and [ 37], which converts flowcharts into source code. The first step in their presented approach involved image preprocessing to obtain the flowchart in a planar perspective, followed by the application of OpenCV contour detection. For shape recognition, the authors applied Tiny YOLOv3 [ 38] in conjunction with Darknet [ 39]—a C and CUDA-based deep neural network (DNN) framework commonly used for implementing YOLO. YOLOv3-SPP (YOLOv3-Spatial Pyramid Pooling) [ 40] was used for lines and arrow recognition, and Google Cloud's Vision API was used for OCR-based text extraction. Graph Models (GM) were constructed to represent the flowchart structure, which were then populated with parse trees for each node to create the final Flowchart Graphs (FGs). All experiments were implemented using Python on Ubuntu 18.04. For the performance evaluation of this work, a new dataset comprising 161 images was created, consisting of 38 digital and 123 hand-drawn flowcharts. Using mAP, their presented method achieved an average precision of 99.82% for shape recognition and 88.14% for arrow and line detection. Although these results are promising, the authors emphasize that further improvements could be achieved by using larger datasets and more advanced models. This approach nevertheless demonstrated significant potential for accurately extracting flowcharts. The authors further suggest that it would be worthwhile to develop a mobile application capable of capturing flowcharts and translating them directly into code. In 2022, Liu et al. [ 41] proposed FlowChart to Code (FC2Code), a system that extracts text from flowcharts, converts it into pseudocode, and generates executable source code from it. Prior work, such as [ 42], introduced RAPTOR, a visual programming tool that helps students translate flowcharts into pseudocode. The FC2Code system used a custom dataset of 320 flowcharts with corresponding source code, of which 50 were used for testing. A rule-based structure recognition algorithm was applied to convert flowcharts into pseudocode, while Bidirectional LSTM [ 43] and Graph Attention Network (GAT) models [ 44] extracted textual information. To maintain information integrity, Reversed Flowchart, GAT, and the TranX decoder [ 45] were utilized to convert pseudocode into executable code. Results were compared against LSTM and transformers [ 46] baselines. Performance was evaluated using the BLEU score, where the proposed method achieved a score of 55.68, compared to 38.25 for LSTM and 44.05 for the transformer baseline approach. As shown, this method significantly outperforms both LSTM and transformer networks. Although effective, the authors note that further work is required to enhance precision and address challenges in integrating flowchart structure into pseudocode. In 2023, the authors of [ 47] introduced a public flowchart dataset named FloCo and a method, FloCo-T5, that converts flowcharts into Python code. This method was evaluated using their dataset, which contains 11,884 flowcharts, including both digital and handwritten ones, with 1188 (10%) used for testing. Their presented approach is based on the work in [ 48], another flowchart dataset but unsuited for programming tasks, as well as the work in [ 23], which converted handwritten flowcharts into executable code. Additionally, it draws inspiration from the seminal work in [ 49], the groundbreaking work that introduced BERT for NLP language representation. Closely related is the work in [ 50], which digitizes handwritten flowcharts using Faster R-CNN and OCR methods, though its dataset lacked corresponding C codes. A Hough transform (HT) [ 51] was employed for shape recognition, EasyOCR was used for text recognition, while Code-T5 [ 52] was employed for source code generation. Handwritten text detection was handled using Character-Region Awareness For Text Detection (CRAFT) [ 53], and TrOCR was used for text recognition. To support their evaluation efforts, data augmentation was performed to increase the size of the available dataset, and a BERT-like Code-T5 masked modeling pre-training strategy was employed to enhance the structural and semantic understanding of code and flowcharts. Positional encoding was added to flowchart encodings before feeding to the transformer encoder. A single NVIDIA A6000 with 48GB VRAM was used for training. On the digital flowchart test set, the proposed method achieved a BLEU score of 67.4 and a CodeBLEU score of 75.7. On hand-drawn flowcharts (40 samples), BLEU and CodeBLEU scores of 21.4 and 34.6 were achieved. Although effective on digital flowcharts, the authors note that extracting hand-drawn flowcharts into Python source code required further study. Future efforts thus aim to expand the dataset with more complex code samples and improve performance on longer programs. In 2024, Darda and Jain introduced a system that extracts flowcharts and generates Python code [ 54]. It builds upon the work in [ 37, 41]. This work was influenced by the preceding efforts in [ 55], which introduced and presented S-DistilBERT, along with Ray et al.'s Flow2Code published in 2019 [ 56] used for processing pictures of handwritten flowcharts and converting them into executable code. Darda and Jain utilized EasyOCR and OpenCV for text extraction, and employed Llama 2 LLM [ 57] for Python code generation via a cloud-based API. Python code generation was successful for 75% of the flowcharts, and the method produced accurate source code, even when 62% of the flowcharts had added noise. However, the authors also note that improvements are needed for more accurate text extraction and to provide multilingual capabilities. As shown by these works, the advancements in LLMs have enabled new techniques for flowchart information extraction, which prove to be quite effective across a range of different imagery. However, significantly more work is needed to improve accuracy and precision, which the following works aim to address. Another study in 2023, Ghosh et al. [ 55] proposed a fine-tuned S-DistilBERT model for matching handwritten flowcharts, pseudocode, and English descriptions. The model combines Sentence-BERT (S-BERT) [ 58] with DistilBERT [ 59], a lightweight variant of BERT [ 49]. Their work builds upon prior research in flowchart recognition, including online handwritten flowchart recognition systems [ 60] and document generation from flowcharts [ 30, 61]. Their experiments utilized 50 handwritten flowcharts, each with manually created pseudocode and descriptions for validation. Binarization, thinning, and polygon detection were used for preprocessing the data, and a third-party text OCR application was used for text recognition. Pseudocode symbols were converted to English equivalents before embedding. The system successfully generated equivalent pseudocode in 85% of cases and achieved matching accuracies of 75.59% for existing pseudocode-to-text pairs and 74.57% for generated pseudocode. This work demonstrated that transformer-based approaches are effective for algorithm-to-text matching tasks. The authors note that recursive flowcharts may be addressed in future work. Several studies published between 2024 and 2025, such as [ 62, 63, 64], all reference FlowLearn [ 65] to enhance dataset annotation and architectural performance. For example, the authors of [ 62] introduced TEXTFLOW, a dual-stage framework for flowchart understanding that decomposes the task into vision textualization and textual reasoning. Building upon prior work in flowchart understanding, such as FlowchartQA [ 48], which emphasizes reasoning over geometric and topological features, the authors addressed limitations in end-to-end VLM approaches by separating visual extraction from logical reasoning. TEXTFLOW was evaluated on FlowVQA [ 66] (2005 QA pairs from 200 sampled flowcharts) and FlowLearn [ 65] (seven tasks with 100 flowchart/QA pairs each) datasets. The framework explores three alternative textual representation formats—MERMAID, GRAPHVIZ, and PLANTUML—with GRAPHVIZ proving most effective. The system utilizes Vision-Language Models (VLMs) such as Claude 3.5 Sonnet [ 67], GPT-4o [ 68], and Qwen2-VL [ 69] for the vision textualization stage, and either VLMs or LLMs for the reasoning stage. Experiments were conducted using 1 to 4 NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs depending on model requirements, with no model training performed. The system achieved state-of-the-art performance, with 82.74% accuracy on FlowVQA and 80.57% on FlowLearn. Notably, tool-assisted methods achieved an accuracy of 88.23% on FlowVQA. The authors note that future work should address extraction accuracy, dataset diversity, and generalizability to more complex diagram types. The authors of [ 70] proposed GenFlowchart, a generative AI-enhanced framework for automated flowchart parsing and understanding. The approach builds upon prior work in flowchart recognition [ 37, 71] and flowchart-grounded task-oriented dialogs [ 72]. GenFlowchart employs a multi-stage pipeline: images are preprocessed via grayscale conversion and binarization, text is extracted using Pytesseract OCR configured with Page Segmentation Mode (PSM) 4 and OCR Engine Mode 3, and geometric shapes are identified using the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [ 73]. The textual and geometric data are then integrated using GPT-3.5 Turbo with prompt engineering techniques (zero-shot and instruction-based prompting). The system was evaluated on a dataset of 550 flowcharts extracted from patent documents using PyMuPDF. Multiple evaluation metrics were employed: manual satisfaction scoring (achieving 4.24/5), BERT Score metrics (with instruction-based prompting achieving BERT-F1 of 86.49), Cosine Similarity with Word2Vec [ 74] (72.38%), and Cosine Similarity with sentence transformers (76.25%). An ablation study demonstrated SAM's critical role, as its removal reduced satisfaction scores from 4.24 to 3.14. The results demonstrated that generative AI significantly enhances flowchart parsing and understanding. In [ 75], the authors present FR-DETR (Flowchart Recognition DEtection TRansformer), an end-to-end multi-task network for machine-generated flowchart recognition. FR-DETR builds upon DEtection TRansformer (DETR) [ 76] and LinE segment TRansformer (LETR) [ 77], combining object detection and line segment detection in a unified framework. The architecture employs a CNN backbone (ResNet-50/101) for feature extraction and a multi-scale transformer structure with shared encoders and separate decoders for encoding and decoding. A coarse-to-fine strategy enables the model to first analyze low-resolution features (1/32 scale) to identify potential target regions, then examine high-resolution features (1/16 scale) for precise detection of symbols and line segments. The model was evaluated on two datasets: CLEF-IP (approximately 60 flowcharts) and a newly constructed dataset of 1000+ flowcharts (800 training, 200 testing) with 25K+ symbol instances and 20K+ line segments. FR-DETR achieved 98.7% precision and 98.1% recall on CLEF-IP (F1-Score: 98.4%), and 94.0% precision with 93.1% recall on the new dataset (F1-Score: 93.5%), outperforming prior methods. However, the authors note that the model's computational requirements limit its applicability for real-time processing and mobile deployment, which remained a direction for future research. [ 61], building on the work shown in [ 60, 78], among others, with the goal of improving upon their overall performance and accuracy. The authors proposed Instance GNN (InstGNN), a framework that represents each flowchart as a graph where nodes represent strokes and edges represent relationships between strokes. The method extracts 27 node features and 19 edge features, then employs GAT for learning. InstGNN performs three tasks: node classification, edge classification, and node embedding learning. Three inference methods were proposed: InstGNN-EC (edge-based), InstGNN-NE (clustering-based), and InstGNN (combining both). Symbol verification uses a ResNet-18 CNN classifier [ 79]. The framework was implemented using Deep Graph Library (DGL) with PyTorch and evaluated on four datasets: FC_A [ 80], FC_B [ 81], FA [ 82], and CASIA-OHFC (2957 flowcharts—a new dataset created by the authors). The method achieved a Stroke Classification Accuracy (SCA) of 95.80% and Symbol Recognition Recall (SRR) of 79.47%, outperforming previous methods. Mermaid achieved 100% compilation success rates (18/18) in optimal configurations, significantly outperforming PlantUML (12/18) and Structurizr (2/18). Future work includes developing agentic AI for dynamic diagram type determination, enhancing usability through improved UI and integration, refining document structuring with flexible rules, strengthening diagram compilation with multi-DSL validation and feedback loops, and expanding the dataset to improve generalization and output quality. Another 2025 study by Johri et al. [ 86] introduced an API-based flowchart-to-code generation system that integrates multiple transformer models. Building upon work in RNN/LSTM limitations and LLM applications [ 102, 103], their architecture employs a pipeline consisting of a Main API coordinating with specialized services: an Image Upload Service for flowchart processing, GPT-4 Vision API for visual content extraction and validation, and a Processing Service API utilizing multiple LLMs (GPT-4o mini and Claude 3.5 Sonnet) for code generation. The system extracts tokens from flowchart elements (decision points, processes, input/output operations) and converts them into functional code across multiple programming languages. The authors selected GPT-4o based on its superior performance on standard benchmarks (Artificial Analysis Quality Index score of 77 and HumanEval coding efficiency of 92%), though they do not report quantitative evaluation results for their own system. The paper demonstrated successful code generation for algorithms including stack insertion, odd/even detection, and merge sort. The authors acknowledged limitations in handling complex or hand-drawn flowcharts and outlined future work to enhance visual recognition capabilities, support additional programming languages, and integrate with development environments and version control systems. Several papers published in 2025 demonstrated that multi-modal LLMs can compete with purpose-built computer vision frameworks for diagram recognition. Otto et al. [ 87] introduced an LLM-based approach for state diagram recognition and code generation in Industry 4.0 contexts. Their pipeline involves manual cropping from PDFs, prompt-engineered GPT-4o for node/edge extraction, and template-based C++ code generation. Evaluated on IEC 61158 (PROFINET, 80 diagrams) and IEC 62541 (OPC UA, 15 diagrams), GPT-4o achieved 68% zero-shot accuracy, outperforming Claude 3 (40%) and Llama 3 (16%). Practical edge recognition reached 63% for PROFINET and 45% for OPC UA, enabling the generation of thousands of lines of boilerplate code and unit tests. The study demonstrated that LLMs generalize across diagram styles without retraining, unlike computer vision models, though they required prompt engineering and manual review for complex diagrams. Finally, the work presented by Pawar [ 104] introduced the Dynamic Flow-to-Code Generator (DFCG), a system for automated flowchart-to-code translation across multiple programming languages. Building upon sketch-based flowchart detection [ 105] and prior code generation frameworks [ 41, 47, 106], the DFCG employs a multi-stage pipeline integrating computer vision and deep learning techniques. The system uses binarization and edge enhancement for preprocessing, Faster R-CNN with a VGG-16 backbone for shape detection, a Region Proposal Network (RPN) for object localization, Keras OCR combined with a CRNN model for text recognition, Probabilistic Hough Transform for arrow detection, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for constructing logical flowchart representations that are then translated into C, C++, Java, and Python code. Evaluated on a dataset of 7500 segmented flowchart symbols (derived from 775 full diagrams) and 100 manually drawn flowcharts, the shape detection module achieved 95.8% for mAP and F1-Score. The end-to-end system demonstrated 95.0% Graph Matching Score (GMS) for flowchart reconstruction and 92.4 BLEU score for code generation, outperforming baselines including FloCo-T5 and FC2Code. Future improvements include expanding language support, integrating LLMs for enhanced code quality, improving handwriting and complex graph recognition, optimizing for mobile deployment, and adding multimodal inputs such as speech-to-code and sketch-based flowchart creation. Review of Identified Block Diagram Papers In 2022, BloSum [ 107] was published for block diagram-to-text generation using OCR and deep learning models. Unlike template-based approaches, such as Chart-Text [ 108], BloSum employed a generic framework without predefined templates. The authors created a Computerized Block Diagram (CBD) dataset containing 502 diagrams (300 training, 106 validation, 96 test) with over 13,000 annotated elements, and extended two handwritten datasets: FC_A (248 training, 171 test) and FC_B (280 training, 196 validation, 196 test) by adding summaries and triplets. The BloSum architecture used Faster R-CNN with FPN [ 109] and Inception-ResNet-v2 [ 110] for shape prediction, EasyOCR for text extraction, Hough Line Transform (HLT) for arrow detection, and a Triplet Generator to convert shape-arrow relationships into <head, relation, tail> format. T5 [ 111] and BART language models, pre-trained on WebNLG 2017 [ 112] and fine-tuned on the block diagram datasets, generated final descriptions. BloSum with T5_Large (770M parameters) achieved BLEU of 42.86 and ROUGE-1 of 81.29 on the CBD dataset. When using ground-truth OCR values, performance on handwritten datasets improved to BLEU of 51.73 and ROUGE-1 of 88.24 on FC_A, and BLEU of 53.17 and ROUGE-1 of 85.56 on FC_B. The authors identified key challenges, including the complexity of accurately detecting arrows, the relatively low accuracy of OCR for handwritten text, hallucinations in language model outputs, and the need for larger-scale datasets. In 2024, another framework for block diagram extraction was introduced called BlockNet [ 113]. This paper referenced BloSum [ 107], due to the similarity of block diagram extraction. The authors stated that BloSum can suffer from hallucinations due to the use of language models. They also pointed out the smaller dataset size of BloSum (502 images total for CBD) as a contributing factor. BlockNet, by comparison, used a dataset of 83,394 images (BD-EnKo), with 75,034 samples used for training. Other related work mentioned involved symbol detection using Faster R-CNN. To create the BD-EnKo dataset used by BlockNet, Mermaid code was utilized for block diagram creation and visualization, along with the addition of noise, blur effects, and grayscale effects to the images to enhance the robustness of the resulting BlockNet approach. GPT-3.5 was used for both generating the Mermaid code and creating text descriptions for the block diagrams. The BD-EnKo dataset comprised 83,394 English and Korean block diagrams, consisting of 46,599 English samples and 36,795 Korean samples across eight distinct diagram types (Flowchart, GraphLR, GraphTD, Journey, Sequence, Simple Graph, State Diagram, and C4), as well as real-world samples. The authors divided their technique into three key components: a local extractor, a global extractor, and an integrator. In the local extractor stage, YOLOv5 was utilized for object detection, trained on the CBD, FC_A, and FC_B datasets. The Pororo OCR package was used due to its support of both English and Korean text. A custom algorithm called BlockSplit divided block diagrams into sub-diagrams containing two connected nodes, utilizing the Triplet Generator to represent shapes, arrows, and their relationships in the format <head, relation, tail>. In the global information extraction stage, the Swin Transformer [ 114] was employed as the visual encoder, and BART was used for text decoding. In the integrator stage, to minimize errors, GPT-4V was utilized to combine local and global information and to output a final summary of the given block diagram. On the CBD dataset, BlockNet achieved a BLEU of 72.31 and a ROUGE-1 of 88.9. On the BD-EnKo English subset, BlockNet achieved a BLEU of 58.61 and a ROUGE-1 of 74.25. This demonstrated that using a larger dataset and a more complex fusion-based framework can achieve higher precision and accuracy. Nonetheless, the reliability of these approaches remains a significant challenge when applied to a wide range of diagram images of varying quality. 4.4.2. The approach employed Faster R-CNN for detecting actors, use cases, and relationships in use case diagrams, and a Single Shot Detector (SSD) model for identifying classes and relationships in class diagrams. For text extraction, the system used Tesseract OCR combined with Keras-OCR for preprocessing. For plagiarism and correctness metrics, formulas based on component matching were proposed, though specific accuracy scores for these metrics were not reported. Another UML sequence diagram extraction approach named Sequence Diagram Extraction (SDE), from [ 124], was inspired by [ 115]. The method first applied image preprocessing techniques, including grayscale conversion and morphological operations, to reduce noise. YOLOv5 was then employed for object detection. OpenCV was used to precisely localize, segment, and crop each detected region. EasyOCR was subsequently applied for text extraction. Finally, a semantic merging process integrated the extracted elements to reconstruct the comprehensive semantics of the sequence diagram. 50 sequence diagrams were used to evaluate the performance of the SDE architecture. The SDE method achieved nearly perfect detection for objects (100% precision, 98% recall, 99% F1-score) and strong performance for messages (87.27% precision, 86.03% recall, 87% F1-score), while detection of complex combined fragments was less reliable (73.33% precision, 70.13% recall, 72% F1-score). GPT-4V significantly outperformed other models with zero syntax errors (0/36 attempts) compared to 44–56% error rates for other LLMs. The study found that diagram complexity negatively affected accuracy and that simpler prompts were more effective. Despite strong results, human validation remained necessary. Future work included extending to other diagram types and implementing automated validation. Finally, Bates et al. [ 125] proposed a method for converting UML diagram images directly into PlantUML code, building on prior works such as [ 100, 115]. Their approach leveraged LLaVA-1.5—a multimodal large language model (MM-LLM) [ 126]—for generating PlantUML code from activity and sequence diagrams. They evaluated four dataset sizes (small, medium, large, and extra-large) using two fine-tuning strategies: standard fine-tuning and Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [ 127]. The models were configured with either 7B or 13B parameters. In evaluations on the extra-large synthetic dataset containing 29,998 sequence diagrams and 30,000 activity diagrams for testing, the LoRA-tuned LLaVA-13B model achieved peak performance metrics of 77.9 for BLEU and 94.2 for Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) [ 128] on sequence diagrams. These results demonstrate that large-scale synthetic training effectively enhances the model's ability to maintain syntactic and structural fidelity. Review of Identified Papers While flowcharts and block diagrams are seen as closely related, electrical circuit diagrams and schematics are significantly different. Image content extraction from electrical circuit diagrams often targets circuit simulators via netlist generation, and thus demands very high extraction accuracy. Netlists are files that utilize a text representation to describe an electrical circuit, including its components, their parameters, and the connections between them. In 2020, the work in [ 129] describes a circuit diagram approach that expands upon the works of [ 130, 131]. Their approach starts by first binarizing the image and performing noise reduction. A morphological 'close' operator is then applied to transform circuit components into blobs. This is followed by localization, to extract circuit components without any of the diagram's wires after applying the Run Length Smoothing Algorithm (RLSA) [ 132]. The authors also generated their own dataset of 60 hand-drawn circuit diagrams, as they were unable to locate any publicly accessible datasets applicable to their research. A localization accuracy evaluation was performed, yielding an overall accuracy of 91.28% in extracting circuit components. The work in [ 133], referred to as Img2Sim and published in 2022, employs an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) approach [ 134] to convert circuit schematics into a netlist. This work builds upon a prior approach published in [ 135] that used deep learning to recognize circuit components, and [ 136], which involved identifying hand-drawn circuit images using OCR. The work published in [ 137] is also referenced. However, while these methods primarily focus on passive components, Img2Sim also aims to support active components. A dataset comprising 330 circuit diagrams was used for testing, utilizing YOLOv5 for element recognition, a Hough Transform for node detection, and OCR for text recognition, with the final results generated as a netlist. Their presented method detects circuit components with an accuracy of 98%, and generates correct netlists with an accuracy of 90%. This demonstrates that, while there is room for improvement in circuit diagram extraction from images, significantly more research is needed to accurately represent these diagrams using netlist files post-extraction, in order to support the intended downstream applications. Another method, presented in 2022 [ 138], utilized a DNN to generate netlists from hand-drawn circuit diagrams, building upon the CNN-based classification approach published in [ 137] and other works such as [ 136, 139, 140, 141]. YOLOv5 was utilized for component recognition, adaptive thresholding and bounding box extraction for terminal recognition, as well as k-means clustering for detecting intersections. The Hough Transform was applied for line detection and node identification. This approach was implemented using PyTorch and subsequently evaluated using a dataset of 154 hand-drawn circuits (augmented to 388 training images). It achieved a mAP score with 0.5 IoU of 98.2% for component detection, 92% accuracy for node recognition, and 80% overall circuit reconstruction accuracy. Their results indicated good extraction performance for simple circuit diagrams using passive components; however, further research was needed to improve the accuracy of more complex diagrams involving active components and integrated circuit components. Additionally, these methods required further evaluation using larger datasets spanning a wider range of circuit diagram representations. The authors of [ 142], published in 2021, developed a method that leverages augmented reality techniques to convert hand-drawn circuit diagrams into other representations using a smartphone. The author's work expands upon [ 143, 144], which used an SVM approach for circuit recognition. Feature extraction is performed using a custom algorithm that combines region proposal using SURF, Eigen, and FAST. Dilation and Hough Transform are also incorporated as part of the processing workflow. A capsule network architecture (CapsNet) [ 145] is used instead of a CNN due to limitations with CNNs, as discussed by the authors in their paper, primarily related to the significantly higher training dataset size required for CNNs compared to CapsNets. The final step in their approach is circuit simulation, which determines whether the extraction produces a viable netlist, concluding with the export of the netlist generated by the previous steps. With a custom dataset comprising 800 circuit diagrams used for evaluation, and utilizing the mAP scoring metric with an IoU threshold of 0.3, the resulting score for this approach is 93.64%. Review of Identified Papers A category of technical illustrations that stands on its own is timing diagrams. Unlike many other diagram types, the importance of line positioning is significantly elevated, and thus it mandates highly accurate extraction to accurately represent signal change timing. A study published in 2023 developed a method named TD-Magic [ 173], designed to translate timing diagram images into formal specifications using a neuro-symbolic approach. The method uses object detection for rising and falling edge detection, OCR for text label extraction, and image processing techniques to capture synchronization patterns. Their approach was inspired by signal digitization work from the biomedical domain, particularly ECG signal extraction from PDF documents [ 174]. Feature recognition was performed using a custom Signal-Edge Detector (SED) module with YOLOv5 for edge detection, and PaddleOCR for text detection and recognition. A Line-and-Arrow-Detection (LAD) module using image processing morphological operations was employed for identifying arrows and annotation lines. Semantic Interpretation (SEI) was used to generate Strict Partial Order (SPO) representations from the extracted features. To overcome the lack of labeled training data, the authors developed L-TD-G, a synthetic timing diagram generator that produced 15,000 labeled training images. The dataset for evaluation included 30 timing diagrams (29 from industry, one manually drawn). On synthetic validation data, the edge detection achieved near-perfect precision and recall, with mAP@0.5 of 99.5%. For industrial timing diagrams, 23 out of 30 (76.7%) were successfully extracted at the structural level, with 15 (50%) achieving complete accuracy at both structural and textual levels. The results demonstrate promise for the automated extraction of formal specifications from timing diagram images. Another timing diagram extraction technique was introduced in 2025, called TD-Interpreter [ 175]. While it was inspired by TD-Magic [ 173], it differed in its approach and scope. TD-Magic focuses on extracting SPO relations as formal specifications, while TD-Interpreter leverages multimodal learning to interpret timing diagrams through a visual question-answering environment. The work was also inspired by LLaVA, a multimodal large language model. TD-Interpreter utilizes a ViT to process images and extract visual features, which are then processed by the LLaVA-based language model for reasoning and response generation. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) was employed for efficient fine-tuning. To address the lack of labeled training data, the authors developed a synthetic data generation workflow, creating a training dataset comprising 4942 timing diagram images for caption-based tasks and 5292 images for reasoning-based tasks. The model was trained on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs and evaluated on a separate test set, achieving a BLEU-4 score of 95.9 and ROUGE-1 score of 96.7. The results demonstrate that fine-tuning lightweight multimodal LLMs on domain-specific synthetic data can achieve highly accurate timing diagram interpretation, significantly outperforming untuned models like GPT-4o. 4.6.2. Thus, among the two, TD-Interpreter is a more promising approach that, given the required computational resources, will be able to achieve significantly better results for downstream applications depending on accurate timing diagram extraction. Additionally, for this survey, we also considered and reviewed a research domain distinct from the technical diagrams that form the primary focus of this survey, but that may shed some light on related image content extraction approaches that have the potential to be applicable to some of the discussed technical diagram types: time series diagrams. Unlike timing diagrams, which specifically capture the discrete behavior of digital or analog signals for hardware verification, time series diagrams more broadly visualize quantitative data trends over time, and are commonly found in financial reports, scientific publications, and within a data analytics context. Recent work in this area [ 176, 177, 178, 179, 180, 181] explored various approaches for extracting features and detecting anomalies from time series data. These methods typically employ convolutional neural networks for spatial feature extraction, recurrent networks such as LSTMs for modeling temporal dependencies, transformer-based architectures for attention mechanisms, and graph neural networks for capturing inter-variable relationships. While these approaches demonstrated effective solutions for time series analysis and anomaly detection, they target a different application domain, only distantly related to the types of technical diagrams examined in this survey, and are therefore included here as related work outside the primary scope of understanding technical diagrams. 4.7. Comparative Observations and Summary Across Illustration Types Based on the reviewed research papers across all four technical diagram types, transformer-based approaches consistently demonstrate strong performance in terms of accuracy, adaptability, and robustness, regardless of diagram type. While these transformer-based approaches are computationally expensive, more lightweight models are available for application scenarios where efficiency outweighs accuracy, such as in edge computing or mobile applications. Object detection frameworks such as YOLO and LLMs such as the various GPT models also offer reliable performance across diverse scenarios. Traditional extraction methods remain useful for specific tasks, but combining them with modern techniques generally yields the most effective results across a wide range of situations. It is important to note that different diagram types attract varying levels of research attention, which influences how rapidly performance improvements can be achieved. Flowcharts are the most extensively examined type of technical diagram, which has led to more advanced, fine-tuned, and higher-accuracy extraction techniques, along with publicly accessible benchmark datasets. Circuit diagrams have also received considerable research focus. However, progress in this area remains constrained by the reliance on small, domain-specific, custom-built datasets. In contrast, timing diagrams, block diagrams, and UML diagrams have received relatively limited attention, leading to slower methodological advancements and lower overall extraction performance. A notable issue identified in the reviewed research is the scarcity of publicly available datasets that can be reliably used for testing and evaluating extraction methods. Most studies relied on custom datasets tailored to their specific needs, which may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of their results. The availability of a standard public dataset could enable more robust and applicable benchmarking, supporting fairness in comparing various image content analysis approaches. 5. Conclusions With the rapid proliferation of LLMs and agentic AI in our everyday lives comes a resurgence in the need for multimodal data access to inform queries and produce accurate responses. As a result, we see growing research activities in image content analysis and extraction for complex technical illustrations, such as flowcharts, block diagrams, UML diagrams, schematics, and timing diagrams—illustrations where a significant portion of the information is represented not just by text, but by the contained shapes, their styling, and their relationships. To cope with increasingly complex diagrams and the need to reduce extraction errors, the techniques utilized for extracting information from these technical illustrations must also evolve. The rise of AI-powered techniques resulted in a number of highly promising image content analysis techniques, achieving higher accuracy and richer contextual representations than ever before, across a variety of diagram types. Our survey found that the majority of recent research activities focused on flowcharts, while circuit schematics represent the second-most studied diagram type. In contrast, block diagrams, UML diagrams, and timing diagrams have received only limited interest, with only two studies dedicated to block diagrams and timing diagrams, and five studies dedicated to UML diagrams. While the advancements made so far are promising, significant work remains as future research avenues to optimize extraction methods for various diagram types, reduce extraction errors, and improve the cost and complexity of these algorithms. Reducing complexity, in particular, is crucial for accelerating information extraction in an era where an ever-increasing amount of multimodal information is being generated. Similarly, for applications where accuracy is paramount, further research is necessary to enhance information extraction in terms of both completeness and accuracy. This is vital for reducing hallucinations and downstream generative errors in agentic AI systems. Finally, we also observed that the significant lack of benchmark datasets severely impedes objective comparisons across the various techniques. While several research works utilize larger datasets with thousands of images, others use much smaller datasets, sometimes comprised of only a few dozen images. Thus, there is a strong need to establish and publicly release benchmark datasets that span the full range of technical diagram types, with sufficient representation in terms of complexity, color usage, multilingual aspects, diagram correctness, and other features. This survey aims to provide both a cross-section of research work related to information extraction from technical diagrams, as well as inspire researchers to pursue the various open research questions in this field, in an effort to advance the accuracy, fidelity, and efficiency of these techniques, because there are numerous applications that would be enabled by these capabilities.
